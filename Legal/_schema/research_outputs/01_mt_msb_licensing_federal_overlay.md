Montana MSBs – State Licensing vs Federal BSA
Obligations
Executive Summary
No State License in Montana: Montana stands alone as the only U.S. state that does not require a
money transmitter or Money Services Business (MSB) license . Under Montana law, “money
transmission” is not even defined . The Montana Division of Banking explicitly states it “does not
regulate money transmitters” .
Only “Pure” Money Transmission Unlicensed: If a company’s only activity in Montana is money
transmission, it needs no Montana license . However, if the business also engages in other
financial services, Montana may require a different license (e.g. lending or escrow – see below) .
Other Montana Licenses May Apply: Montana does license related financial activities. For example,
firms making consumer loans or collecting loan payments must have a consumer finance license;
companies providing escrow services must be licensed; Montana also licenses retail installment
finance companies, mortgage lenders, and charters banks/credit unions . An MSB with activities
beyond pure transmission (like making loans or handling escrow funds) could fall under one of these
licensing regimes.
Regulator Stance: Montana’s Division of Banking acknowledges the “challenge” of being the only
state with no MSB license. In 2023 it briefly requested business plans from crypto exchanges and
money transmitters, but withdrew that request after recognizing it had no authority over
unlicensed MSBs . Montana continues to rely on federal oversight for money transmitters
.
Federal Law Still Applies: A Montana-based MSB is fully subject to federal Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)
and FinCEN regulations. Not needing a state license does not exempt a business from federal MSB
registration and compliance . FinCEN requires MSBs nationwide to register and implement
anti-money laundering programs regardless of state licensing .
“Money Transmitter” Definition (FinCEN): Under federal regulation, a money transmitter is any
person or business that accepts currency or other value from one person and transmits it to
another person or location by any means . This broad definition covers traditional remitters
and cryptocurrency exchanges alike (FinCEN deems convertible virtual currency as “other value”
substituting for currency) . The rule also includes any business “engaged in the transfer of
funds” . Exceptions: FinCEN excludes those who only transport cash, only provide the network or
communication services, act as mere payment processors for goods/services, or deal solely in
prepaid access (stored value) . Individuals who perform such services infrequently and not for
profit are also not MSBs .
FinCEN MSB Registration (Form 107): All MSBs (including money transmitters) must register with
FinCEN. Initial registration must be filed within 180 days of starting business using FinCEN Form 107
. Registration must be renewed every 2 years by December 31 of the appropriate year . A
Montana MSB is not excused from registration – state licensure is separate from the federal
requirement . (Montana’s lack of a license means 18 U.S.C. §1960’s state-license prong doesn’t
apply, but failure to register federally is itself a crime, as noted below.)

Registration Exemptions: An MSB that is solely an agent of another MSB (e.g. a small grocery
store acting as an agent of Western Union) does not have to register independently . Also,
businesses that only issue or sell stored-value (prepaid) products are exempt from registration
. The U.S. Postal Service and government agencies are exempt by statute .
AML Program Requirement: Every MSB must implement a written, risk-based anti-money
laundering (AML) compliance program . The program must include four pillars: (1) internal
policies, procedures, and controls to ensure BSA compliance (including customer identification, BSA
reporting, recordkeeping, and law enforcement information requests) ; (2) designation of a
compliance officer responsible for day-to-day compliance and program updates ; (3) ongoing
training for relevant personnel on BSA/AML duties (e.g. detecting suspicious activity) ; and (4)
independent review of the program (periodic audit/testing by an independent party or staff not
involved in compliance) to ensure the AML program’s effectiveness . The AML program must be
commensurate with the MSB’s size and risk and must be available to Treasury/FinCEN upon request
.
Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs): Money transmitters (and most MSBs) are required to file SARs
with FinCEN for any transaction (or pattern) of $2,000 or more that the MSB knows, suspects, or has
reason to suspect is suspicious – e.g. involves illegal funds, is designed to evade BSA rules (like
structuring transactions), or lacks lawful purpose . The $2,000 threshold applies to MSBs such
as money transmitters, currency exchangers, and issuers of money orders/traveler’s checks .
(Check cashers are not mandated to file SARs unless they offer other MSB services .) SARs must
be filed within 30 calendar days of detecting the suspicious facts , and supporting records must
be retained for 5 years. Filing is done electronically using the FinCEN SAR form. Importantly, SARs
(and the fact an SAR was filed) must be kept confidential by the MSB.
Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs): Like other financial institutions, MSBs must file a CTR (FinCEN
Form 112) for any cash transaction exceeding $10,000 in one day, whether one transaction or
multiple that total over $10k (e.g. a customer sends three $4,000 transfers in one day) . The CTR
reports the details of the customer and transaction to FinCEN. The $10,000 threshold is mandated by
31 CFR 1010.311 and applies to deposits, withdrawals, exchanges of currency or payments/
transfers over that amount . CTRs generally must be e-filed within 15 days of the transaction.
(Structuring transactions to avoid CTR filing is a federal crime, and MSBs should monitor for such
behavior.)
Recordkeeping – Funds Transfers (Travel Rule): For any funds transmittal of $3,000 or more,
MSBs must obtain and retain certain key records (the “Travel Rule” under 31 CFR 1010.410(e)). At a
minimum, the transmitting MSB must record the name and address of the sender, the date and
amount of the transfer, the beneficiary’s financial institution, and as available the name and address
of the recipient (plus account number or other identifier if provided) . If the sender or
recipient is not an established customer, the MSB must also verify and record the person’s identity
(e.g. by examining a photo ID for in-person transfers) . These details must “travel” with the
payment order to the next institution and be retained. The MSB receiving funds for payout to a non-
customer must similarly verify the recipient’s identity for amounts ≥$3k . Records Retention: All
BSA-required records (including the above and copies of CTRs/SARs filed) must be kept for 5 years
and be available for inspection .
Enforcement – Civil Penalties: FinCEN can impose significant civil penalties for MSB violations.
Operating an MSB without registering with FinCEN can incur a civil penalty up to $5,000 per
violation per day of non-compliance . FinCEN has penalized businesses for failure to register and
for AML program violations. For example, in 2017 FinCEN fined BTC-e (a now-defunct offshore crypto
exchange) $110 million for willfully violating BSA requirements – including operating as an

Enforcement – Criminal Liability: Operating an “unlicensed” money transmitting business is a
federal felony. Under 18 U.S.C. § 1960, anyone who knowingly conducts a money transmission
business that either (a) lacks a required state license, (b) fails to register with FinCEN, or (c) transmits
funds known to be criminal proceeds, can be prosecuted . Conviction carries up to 5 years
imprisonment and fines . Even in Montana (no state license requirement), an MSB that fails to
register with FinCEN or comply with BSA could be prosecuted under prong (b) of §1960 for operating
without federal registration . DOJ Enforcement: The U.S. Department of Justice aggressively
pursues unregistered or illicit MSBs. For example, in 2025 the DOJ announced that OKX, a major
offshore crypto exchange, pleaded guilty to criminal charges of operating an unlicensed money
transmitting business after it willfully served U.S. customers without FinCEN registration or an AML
program. OKX agreed to pay over \$500 million and admitted it allowed over \$5 billion in suspicious
transactions by failing to follow BSA rules . Similarly, individual peer-to-peer crypto traders
have been convicted under §1960 for failing to obtain MSB registration or state licenses. These cases
underscore that Montana businesses are not immune – federal law applies nationwide.
Open Questions: Because Montana provides no MSB license or definition, businesses must self-
assess which (if any) Montana financial license might apply to their full business model. This can be
complex – e.g. does a crypto exchange also engage in lending or custodial activities that trigger
Montana’s banking or consumer credit laws? The Montana Division of Banking has suggested
companies submit their business plans to determine if other licenses are needed , but since it
lacks jurisdiction over pure money transmitters, formal guidance is limited. Another open area is
whether Montana might adopt the Money Transmission Modernization Act (a model law) in the
future – as of 2025, Montana has not done so, but this could change, requiring monitoring. All
MSBs in Montana should regularly verify their federal compliance (registration renewals, AML audits,
etc.) and watch for any state law changes that could introduce licensing.
Montana Licensing Determination for Money Transmitters
No State License Required: Montana imposes no state licensing requirement on money transmitters or
MSBs. In contrast to every other U.S. state, Montana has no Money Transmitter Act. The Montana Division
of Banking and Financial Institutions plainly states on its official website: “MONTANA DOES NOT REGULATE
MONEY TRANSMITTERS” . There is no Montana statute defining money transmission or requiring a
license for it . A company engaging solely in money transmission can operate in Montana without a
state license (though it must still comply with federal law, discussed below).
Scope of Unlicensed Activity: Notably, Montana’s lack of regulation applies only if “the only thing you are
doing is money transmission” . Since “money transmission” is not defined under Montana law , this
generally means activities like receiving money for transfer or issuing payment instruments are unregulated
by the state. For example, a Montana-based cryptocurrency exchange or a payments startup that only
facilitates transfers of funds (fiat or crypto) would not need a Montana license (while in any other state it
likely would). This regulatory gap has made Montana attractive to some fintech and crypto companies as a
“safe harbor” from state MTL licensing .

Other Montana Laws Still Apply: Importantly, the Division of Banking cautions that just because money
transmission itself isn’t licensed, it “absolutely does not mean” an MSB needs no Montana licenses if it
conducts other regulated activities . Montana regulates a variety of financial services, and an MSB’s
broader business model might fall into one of these categories. For instance:
Consumer Loan Companies: Montana’s Consumer Loan Act (administered by the Division of
Banking) requires a license for businesses making or servicing consumer loans. If an MSB also offers
credit (like payday loans or lines of credit) or engages in debt collection on loans, it must be
licensed accordingly .
Escrow and Money Handling: Companies that provide escrow services or hold client funds for
release upon conditions must be licensed as escrow businesses in Montana . An MSB that
escrowed customer funds (e.g. for a fiat-to-crypto escrow service) could trigger this license
requirement.
Retail Installment Financing: Montana licenses “sales finance” companies that purchase retail
installment contracts or otherwise finance retail sales . If an MSB extends credit for consumer
purchases or finances transactions, it might need this license.
Mortgage and Banking Services: Offering any banking services, deposit accounts, or mortgage
lending/brokering would implicate Montana’s bank or mortgage licensing laws – though most
pure MSBs avoid these activities. Montana also charters banks and credit unions, but an MSB acting
only as a funds transmitter would not be a depository institution.
In summary, Montana’s policy is that a “pure” money transmission business is exempt from state
licensing, but any additional financial functions are subject to the usual state licensing . The
Division advises companies to consult counsel to determine if their activities fit into one of the existing
license categories . All Montana financial licenses are managed via the Nationwide Multistate Licensing
System (NMLS) .
Regulator Perspective: The Montana Division of Banking is aware that the state’s non-regulation of money
transmitters is unique. In an April 2023 bulletin titled “The Challenge of Being the Only State Not Regulating
Money Transmitters,” the Division’s staff acknowledged that many companies inquire about what licenses
they need . Because Montana had no money transmitter law, the Division at one point requested
voluntary submissions of business plans and fund flow diagrams from money transmitters to assess their
activities . However, the Division’s legal team concluded the agency had no authority to compel
information from unlicensed transmitters, and the request was withdrawn . The incident (cheekily
dubbed a “pump fake” by commentators ) highlights that Montana’s regulators rely on federal
oversight (FinCEN) and indirect monitoring for MSBs, absent state licensing.
No Montana Definition of Money Transmission: Unlike other states’ statutes, Montana’s code does not
define “money transmission” or “money services business.” A search of Montana Code Annotated finds no
dedicated Money Transmitters Act. (The term “money order” is defined for unclaimed property purposes
, but that is unrelated to any licensing requirement.) The absence of definitions means Montana has not
codified what activities count as money transmission. Thus, companies get little statutory guidance on
whether their business is “only money transmission” or strays into something else. This ambiguity
reinforces the need to analyze the specifics of the business against Montana’s other financial laws.
Potential Future Changes: As of 2025, Montana has not adopted the model Money Transmission
Modernization Act (a uniform law many states are enacting to update money transmitter regulations) .

Federal Compliance Obligations for Montana MSBs
Even without a state license requirement, a Montana-based money services business must fully comply
with federal MSB laws and regulations. The U.S. Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), 31 U.S.C. § 5311 et seq., and its
implementing regulations (31 C.F.R. Chapter X) impose a comprehensive compliance regime on MSBs
nationwide. FinCEN (the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, a bureau of the U.S. Treasury) is the federal
regulator for MSB compliance. Below is an outline of the key federal obligations and how they apply to an
MSB operating from Montana:
Federal Definition of “Money Transmitter” and MSB Status
Under federal regulations, a “money services business” (MSB) includes several categories of non-bank
financial institutions, one of which is the money transmitter . FinCEN’s definition of “money
transmitter” is very broad. Essentially, a money transmitter is a person or entity engaged in the business of
accepting money (or its equivalent value) from one party and transmitting it to another party or
location . Specifically, 31 C.F.R. § 1010.100(ff)(5) provides in part:
Money transmitter – (i) In general. (A) A person that provides money transmission services.
The term “money transmission services” means the acceptance of currency, funds, or
other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission of
currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another location or
person by any means.
In short, if an MSB accepts funds from a customer and then sends, transfers, or pays out an equivalent
value to another person or place, it is acting as a money transmitter . This covers traditional activities like
wire remittances, electronic payments, and emerging activities like exchanging or transferring
cryptocurrency (which FinCEN considers “other value…substitutes for currency”) . The rule also includes
as a money transmitter “any other person engaged in the transfer of funds” , a catch-all ensuring that
novel methods of moving value fall under regulation.
FinCEN’s regulations carve out a few limitations/exceptions (31 C.F.R. § 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii)). The term money
transmitter does not include a person that only:
Provides the network, software, or communication services used by a money transmitter (e.g. an
internet service provider or phone company isn’t an MSB just because its network is used for
payments) ;
Acts as a payment processor for goods or services via clearance and settlement with the seller’s
agreement (this covers processors who facilitate payments strictly for purchases of non-financial
goods/services under arrangements with the merchant) ;
Operates a system that transfers funds only between BSA-regulated institutions (e.g. an interbank
clearinghouse) ;

Additionally, banks and persons registered with the SEC or CFTC are excluded from the MSB definition ,
and an individual who occasionally transmits money not as a business (infrequently and without profit

For a Montana business, determining if it’s an MSB (specifically a money transmitter) is the first step. Given
FinCEN’s definitions, most business models that involve handling customer funds and sending value to
others (including exchanging one form of value for another) will qualify as an MSB. For example, a
Montana-based crypto exchange that takes custody of customers’ crypto or fiat and sends equivalent value
to others (like transferring crypto on behalf of users) is a money transmitter under federal law . Likewise,
a check cashing store that also does Western Union transfers is an MSB (both as a check casher and
transmitter). Crucially, Montana’s lack of a state license does not change the federal classification:
FinCEN regulations apply equally to a money transmitter in Montana as in any other state .
FinCEN Registration of MSBs
Once a business meets an MSB definition (e.g. money transmitter, currency exchanger, etc.), it must register
with FinCEN unless an exemption applies. Federal registration is mandated by the BSA, 31 U.S.C. § 5330
and 31 C.F.R. § 1022.380. Key points about MSB registration:
Initial Registration Timing: A new MSB must register within 180 days of starting operations .
FinCEN Form 107, “Registration of Money Services Business,” is the form used to register . This
form collects information about the business (owners, services, locations, etc.). Even if an MSB is
perfectly legal under Montana law, it is unlawful under federal law to operate more than 180 days
without filing this registration .
Renewal Every Two Years: MSB registrations are not one-and-done; they expire and must be
renewed biennially. Specifically, an MSB must renew its registration every 2 calendar years, by
December 31 of the renewal year . For example, if a business initially registered in 2024, it must
renew by December 31, 2026, then again by December 31, 2028, and so on . The renewal is done
by refiling Form 107 with the “renewal” box checked . Failing to renew means the MSB is no
longer in an active registered status after the deadline passes. (FinCEN in practice maintains a public
list of registered MSBs, and businesses that don’t renew are eventually dropped and noted as
unregistered .)
No State License Needed for Registration: FinCEN registration is required whether or not the
MSB has a state license . FinCEN explicitly notes that MSBs must register “whether or not they
are licensed…by any state” . So a Montana MSB gets no pass – it must register with FinCEN as
long as it meets the MSB definitions.

Exemptions from Registration: Certain MSBs are exempt only from the federal registration
requirement (they must still follow other BSA rules like AML programs). The primary exemptions: (1)
an MSB that is solely an agent of another MSB is not required to register ; (2) an MSB that is only
a seller or issuer of stored value (prepaid access) is not required to register ; and (3) the U.S.
Postal Service and government entities are not required to register . For example, if a small
Montana grocery store only sells MoneyGram money orders as an agent of MoneyGram and does no
other MSB activity, it need not register itself . But if that store also cashes checks or does
transfers on its own, it then must register.
Consequences of Not Registering: Operating an MSB without timely registration can lead to heavy
penalties. Civilly, FinCEN can impose a penalty up to $5,000 per day of violation . Criminally, as
noted, 18 U.S.C. § 1960 makes it a felony to willfully fail to register (discussed under Enforcement
below). FinCEN relies on MSB registration as a cornerstone of BSA compliance – it is how the
government knows who is in the business and where to send examiners or subpoenas. Thus,
registration is a fundamental obligation for a Montana MSB.
Practical note: Registration is done online via the BSA E-Filing system. After initial registration, an MSB
must also re-file Form 107 if certain changes occur (e.g. a change in ownership or control, transfer of 10% or
more of ownership, etc., within 180 days of the change) . Additionally, MSBs are required to prepare and
maintain a list of their agents (if they have agents or franchises) and make it available to regulators upon
request (31 C.F.R. § 1022.380(d)). In Montana, an MSB might not have state regulators looking over its
shoulder, but FinCEN and IRS (which examines MSBs on FinCEN’s behalf) can request the agent list and
other records during compliance examinations.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Program Requirements
All MSBs, including the smallest money transmitters, must establish and implement an anti-money
laundering program tailored to their business. This has been a requirement since the early 2000s (USA
PATRIOT Act amendments to the BSA). 31 C.F.R. § 1022.210 is the regulation that mandates MSBs have AML
programs. Key elements:
Written Program, Risk-Based: The AML program must be in writing and approved by
management . It should be “effective” – meaning reasonably designed to prevent the MSB
from being used for money laundering or terrorist financing . The rule explicitly says the
program should be commensurate with the risks related to the MSB’s services, customers, volume,
and geographies . A small local MSB in Montana might have a simpler program than a large
global exchanger, but both must cover certain basics.
Minimum Four Requirements: At a minimum, the program for an MSB must include :
Internal Policies, Procedures and Controls to assure compliance with the BSA . These should
cover procedures for verifying customer identification (especially for transactions that require it),
filing required reports (SARs, CTRs, etc.), creating and retaining records required by law, and
responding to law enforcement requests (e.g. handling subpoenas or Section 314(a) information
requests) . If the MSB uses automation (software for transaction monitoring, etc.), the controls
should integrate with those systems . For MSBs with agent relationships, the responsibilities for

Compliance Officer: The MSB must designate an individual responsible for day-to-day compliance

Ongoing Training: The MSB must provide for education and training of appropriate personnel on

Independent Review/Audit: The MSB must have an independent review of its AML program to test

program’s daily operation . For example, a Montana MSB might hire a consultant or have a

correctly, etc. The key is “independent” – the compliance officer shouldn’t audit their own work.
These four pillars for MSBs mirror the requirements for banks, though scaled for MSBs. FinCEN guidance
(and the FFIEC BSA/AML Exam Manual for MSBs) provides insight into best practices. Common
expectations include that MSBs have written Customer Identification Procedures (especially if they deal in
areas like foreign transfers where verifying a customer’s identity might be required for certain thresholds),
risk-based procedures for enhanced due diligence on higher-risk customers, and procedures to handle
regulatory updates (like new SAR filing instructions or new watchlists).
For a Montana MSB, implementing a solid AML program is crucial. Without state exams, the first external
check on their compliance will likely be an IRS BSA examination (the IRS Small Business/Self-Employed
division conducts exams of MSBs for FinCEN). If deficiencies are found – say the MSB lacked a written
program or wasn’t filing SARs – the MSB could face penalties or enforcement action. Thus, even in
Montana’s light-touch state environment, federal AML program rules require proactive effort by the MSB to
maintain compliance.
SAR Reporting (Suspicious Activity Reports)
Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) are a cornerstone of the BSA regime. MSBs that are money transmitters
(as well as those that are currency exchangers or issue money orders, etc.) are required to monitor
transactions and file SARs for anything suspicious above a certain dollar threshold. The requirement for
MSBs is found at 31 C.F.R. § 1022.320.
Who Must File SARs: FinCEN’s rules state that every MSB described in 31 C.F.R. § 1010.100(ff)(1)-(7) (which
covers money transmitters, currency dealers/exchangers, issuers/sellers of traveler’s checks and money
orders, and the U.S. Postal Service) must file SARs . The only MSB categories exempt from mandatory
SAR filing are check cashers and providers of prepaid access (stored value) . Thus, a Montana money
transmitter definitely falls under the SAR rule. (If an MSB is dual-purpose – e.g. both a transmitter and a
check casher – it must file SARs for its money transmission activity in any case .)

Threshold and Timing: An MSB must file a SAR for any transaction or group of related transactions that
involves $2,000 or more (or $5,000 or more in some special cases for issuers reviewing clearance records
) and that the MSB “knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect” meets one of the suspicious criteria .
The criteria are the standard ones used across financial institutions : - Funds are derived from illegal
activity or the transaction is intended to hide or disguise funds from illegal activity (e.g. structuring
money transfers to avoid reporting, or proceeds of fraud being sent through the MSB). - The transaction is
designed to evade BSA regulations (e.g. a customer splits a $5,000 transfer into three $1,800 transfers
on the same day to avoid the $2k SAR threshold). - The transaction has no business or apparent lawful
purpose, and the MSB cannot determine a reasonable explanation after examining the facts (often a
“red flag” scenario). - Or the transaction involves use of the MSB to facilitate criminal activity (this fourth
category is sometimes explicit in the rules) .
If such suspicious activity is detected, the MSB must file a SAR within 30 calendar days of the initial
detection of facts that may warrant a SAR . This is the same 30-day rule that banks follow. If the suspect
identity is not immediately known, the MSB can extend up to 60 days while trying to identify the suspect,
but generally not beyond that.
Confidentiality and Safe Harbor: SAR filings are confidential; the MSB (and its employees) cannot disclose
to the subject of a SAR that one has been filed. They are protected by law with safe harbor from liability for
filing required or voluntary SARs in good faith. A Montana MSB should train its staff on detecting and
escalating suspicious cases (e.g. multiple customers using the same ID, attempts to send funds just under
thresholds, etc.) and on the sensitivity of SAR information.
Voluntary SARs: If a transaction is below $2,000 but seems suspicious, MSBs can still file a SAR voluntarily
. Also, as FinCEN’s guidance notes, if an MSB has both SAR-covered and non-covered lines (like check
cashing), it may choose to report suspicious activity in the non-covered line even if not required . For
example, if a Montana MSB mainly does money transfers (must file SARs) and also cashes checks (SAR not
required for that), it might still file a SAR if it notices suspicious check cashing tied to the same customer
who is sending money.
Storage and Retention: Copies of filed SARs and all supporting documentation (e.g. transaction records,
identification info, internal notes) must be retained for 5 years from the date of filing (per 31 C.F.R.
§ 1010.430) . These documents are often requested by law enforcement. In an exam, the MSB will need
to produce its SAR filings and show its SAR decision process.
Montana Consideration: Montana authorities do not directly receive SARs (they go to FinCEN’s database),
but federal and state law enforcement agencies can access them. A Montana MSB should not get
complacent due to lack of state oversight – failing to file required SARs can lead to federal penalties. For
instance, FinCEN has penalized MSBs for ignoring obvious red flags (e.g. structuring by customers) and not
filing SARs. A robust AML program as described above is critical to meeting the SAR obligation.
Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs)
A Currency Transaction Report (CTR) is required by federal law for any cash transaction exceeding
$10,000. This applies to all financial institutions under the BSA, which includes MSBs. The general rule at
31 C.F.R. § 1010.311 states that each financial institution (other than casinos) must file a report of each
deposit, withdrawal, exchange of currency, or other payment/transfer involving more than $10,000 in

currency . For MSBs, this typically comes up in two scenarios: - A customer brings in over $10,000 in

The rule also aggregates multiple smaller transactions that the MSB knows are by or on behalf of the same

send each time (total $12,000), the MSB must file a CTR if it is aware that it’s the same customer (common
with structured transactions).
CTR Filing: MSBs file CTRs electronically to FinCEN (FinCEN Form 112). The CTR must include identifying
information about the individual conducting the transaction (name, address, ID, SSN, etc.) and the
beneficiary if different. The filing deadline is within 15 days after the transaction (if e-filed; 25 days if by
paper, though virtually all must be e-filed now).
Exemptions: The concept of exempt persons (for banks, e.g. businesses that regularly deposit cash) doesn’t
directly apply to MSBs in the same manner. Most MSB transactions are by individual customers, not eligible
for exemption. So practically, a Montana MSB will need to file a CTR whenever a large cash deal occurs.
However, in Montana, an MSB might encounter fewer >$10k cash transactions simply due to population and
business size – but they must be prepared for the possibility.
Overlap with SAR: If a transaction is over $10k in cash and is suspicious, the MSB must file both a CTR (for
the currency aspect) and a SAR (for the suspicious aspect) . Filing one does not exempt the other if both
criteria are met. Conversely, if a transaction over $10k is suspicious, you don’t avoid the CTR – you do both
filings.
Recordkeeping Requirements (Transmittals and Other Records)
Besides reporting, MSBs have to maintain various records under the BSA. Two critical recordkeeping rules
for MSBs are: - The “Travel Rule” recordkeeping for fund transfers ≥ $3,000 (31 C.F.R. § 1010.410(e)), and
- The recordkeeping for monetary instrument sales for $3,000–$10,000 (31 C.F.R. § 1010.415), if the MSB
sells money orders, traveler’s checks, etc. for cash. (The latter may apply if a Montana MSB sells money
orders; they must log purchases of $3k–$10k even though no CTR is required under $10k.)
Travel Rule ($3,000+ Transfers): We touched on this under the name/ID information required for
transmittals. When an MSB initiates or receives a payment order (transmittal of funds) of $3,000 or more,
the rule requires collecting and retaining specific information. In summary: - For an outgoing transmittal
(the MSB is the transmittor’s financial institution): The MSB must obtain and keep the sender’s name,
address, identification if not an established customer (and record the ID details), the amount and date of
the transfer, the recipient’s name and address (if provided – most systems will have at least a name for the
beneficiary), the recipient’s account number or a unique reference if available, and the name of the
recipient’s financial institution . Any additional info provided by the sender (e.g. message or purpose)
should be kept as well. If the sender is not a known customer, the MSB must verify their identity via
acceptable ID and record the ID type/number and if not the true transmittor, also record the actual
transmittor’s identifying info . - The MSB must also include certain information in the payment
message to the next institution: at least the sender’s name, account (if any), address, the amount and date,
the recipient’s institution, and any recipient details it has . (This is to ensure the information “travels”
with the funds transfer for transparency.) - For an incoming transmittal (the MSB is paying out to a

recipient’s financial institution) must verify the recipient’s identity (if in person, check ID) and record their

to someone without knowing who they are. If the payout is not in person (e.g. mailed), records of the

The MSB must keep these records for at least 5 years. Also, all such records should be readily retrievable
by reference to the customer’s name or account if applicable . In practice, MSBs use either electronic
systems or paper logbooks to satisfy the travel rule. For example, a Western Union agent in Montana will
input sender and receiver info for transfers – that data is stored and can be produced as required.
Monetary Instrument Log ($3k-$10k purchases): If the MSB sells instruments (like money orders,
traveler’s checks) for cash amounts of $3,000 to $10,000, it must keep a log of such sales, recording
purchaser identification details, date, amount, and instruments’ serial numbers . This is analogous to the
travel rule, but for instrument sales, because no CTR is required under $10k but law enforcement wanted a
record of significant instrument purchases. For a pure money transmitter, this may not apply unless they
also sell money orders.
General Record Retention: As noted, five years is the magic number for BSA record retention. 31 C.F.R.
§ 1010.430 requires that all records required by the BSA (including those above, plus copies of filed CTRs,
SARs, customer receipts, etc.) be kept for 5 years . The 5-year clock generally starts from the date of the
transaction or the filing of the report. In any investigation or exam, FinCEN or IRS will expect the MSB to
produce records going back five years.
For a Montana MSB, maintaining organized records is important since there’s no routine state examination
that might catch problems early. A federal examination could occur years into operation, and the MSB will
need to have retained the necessary historical data. Good recordkeeping also protects the MSB – for
example, being able to show who a customer was and what they did could help refute an accusation that
the MSB was complicit in illegal transfers.
Enforcement Risks and Examples
While Montana might not knock on the MSB’s door, federal agencies certainly will if an MSB fails to comply
with BSA requirements. There are two main enforcement channels: civil enforcement by FinCEN (or
delegated to IRS), and criminal enforcement by the Department of Justice (often working with the FBI,
IRS-CI, or Homeland Security Investigations). We highlight both, with real examples relevant to MSBs:
FinCEN Civil Enforcement
Regulatory Examinations: FinCEN oversees MSBs and has delegated authority to the IRS to conduct BSA
compliance exams on its behalf. If an MSB is found in violation – say it didn’t register, has no AML program,
fails to file SARs/CTRs, or keeps poor records – FinCEN can take administrative action. Often, for less
egregious issues, this may result in a warning or required remedial actions. For serious or willful violations,
FinCEN can assess civil monetary penalties.

Failure to Register Penalties: FinCEN has explicitly warned that it will penalize businesses that flout the
MSB registration requirement. By law, failing to register (31 U.S.C. 5330) carries up to $5,000 per violation
per day of operation unregistered . FinCEN has a track record of penalizing small MSBs for operating
unregistered. For example, FinCEN’s enforcement database shows actions such as: - In the Matter of
Kustandy Rayyan d/b/a Thriftway Food Mart (2016) – a case where a seller of money orders was fined for
operating as an unregistered MSB . - In the Matter of Lee’s Snack Shop (2015) – another convenience store
MSB fined for not registering and failing to implement AML measures .
These cases often involve a penalty on the order of tens of thousands of dollars, depending on the duration
of unregistered activity and volume.
AML Program and Reporting Violations: FinCEN also brings actions for not having an effective AML
program, not filing required SARs/CTRs, and other BSA breaches. Penalties can be substantial, especially if
the violations are “willful” (meaning the MSB knew or should have known of the obligations and ignored
them). Notable examples: - Ripple Labs (2015): This was a high-profile case involving a virtual currency
company. FinCEN fined Ripple Labs Inc. \$700,000 for acting as an MSB (selling a cryptocurrency, XRP)
without registering and without an adequate AML program or filing SARs . Ripple had to register and
beef up its compliance after this enforcement. - Thomas E. Haider/MoneyGram (2014): FinCEN penalized
the former Chief Compliance Officer of MoneyGram, Thomas Haider, \$1 million for willful AML program
failures – a message that individuals can be held accountable . This was tied to MoneyGram agents
engaging in fraud and Haider’s failure to implement effective controls. - BTC-e (2017): FinCEN’s $110 million
penalty against BTC-e (an offshore crypto exchange) mentioned earlier was due to willful violations: BTC-e
had virtually no AML controls and facilitated ransomware and dark web funds flows . FinCEN’s action was
coordinated with DOJ (which indicted BTC-e’s operator; see below).
In civil cases, FinCEN can also use its authority to bar individuals from working in the financial industry. For
instance, as part of settlements, MSB owners/officers might agree not to serve in compliance roles if they
egregiously violated the BSA.
For a Montana MSB, the takeaway is that federal penalties can far outweigh any hassle saved by not
having a state license. It is essential to treat BSA compliance seriously. FinCEN has encouraged reporting
of unregistered MSBs – both by other businesses and by law enforcement . An MSB operating in
Montana that serves customers in other states might also come onto other regulators’ radar (e.g. state
banking departments elsewhere might notify FinCEN of a Montana firm doing business with their residents
without licenses, although Montana itself requires none).
DOJ Criminal Enforcement
On the criminal side, the key statute is 18 U.S.C. § 1960, the federal prohibition on unlicensed money
transmitting businesses. There are also the general money laundering statutes (18 U.S.C. § 1956 and § 1957)
which can apply if the MSB is moving illicit funds, and fraud statutes if the MSB defrauds customers. But
focusing on §1960:
18 U.S.C. § 1960 – Unlicensed Money Transmitting: This law makes it a crime to operate a money
transmitting business without a required state license or without registering with FinCEN, or to
transmit money known to be criminal funds . Conviction is punishable by up to 5 years in prison

State License Prong (1960(b)(1)(A)): Not applicable in Montana (since Montana requires no license).
But note: if a Montana MSB transmits money from other states where a license would be required,
DOJ could assert a violation on that basis. Generally, prong (A) triggers if the business operates in a
state where it is supposed to be licensed but is not. If our Montana MSB has customers in, say,
Washington or California without those state licenses, that could potentially invoke 1960. This is a
complex jurisdictional issue, but worth noting for multi-state operations.
Federal Registration Prong (1960(b)(1)(B)): This absolutely can apply in Montana. If an MSB fails to
register with FinCEN as required, DOJ can charge §1960. They must prove the business knowingly
failed to register while money transmitting in interstate commerce. Many cases have been brought
under this prong, especially against peer-to-peer bitcoin traders who didn’t register.
Criminal Funds Prong (1960(b)(1)(C)): If the MSB is moving money it knows comes from crime (or is
intended for criminal use), DOJ can charge this, which doesn’t depend on licensing at all. But usually
if that’s proven, they might also charge traditional money laundering statutes.
DOJ Case Examples: - In February 2025, the DOJ (SDNY) announced OKX, a major crypto exchange, pleaded
guilty to a §1960 charge for operating without FinCEN registration (and without an AML program). OKX
admitted it knowingly served U.S. customers and facilitated criminal transactions while unregistered
. They paid over \$500M and agreed to compliance improvements. This is a stark warning that even
large international firms can face U.S. prosecution if they ignore the BSA. - The DOJ has prosecuted
numerous smaller MSBs: e.g., a Massachusetts man, Trung Nguyen, was convicted in 2023 for operating an
unlicensed money transmitting business by selling Bitcoin for cash without a license or FinCEN registration
. He also was convicted of money laundering conspiracy because some funds were tied to illicit drug
proceeds. - Another notable case: Larry Dean Harmon, who ran a bitcoin mixing service (“Helix”), was
indicted under §1960 for operating an unlicensed MSB and laundering money. In 2021 he pleaded guilty;
separately FinCEN had fined him \$60 million for BSA violations (one of the first actions against a crypto
mixer). - Tornado Cash (2023): While mainly a sanctions case, the DOJ charged the founders of Tornado
Cash (a crypto mixer on Ethereum) with money laundering and operating an unlicensed money transmitting
business , arguing that even decentralized protocols can count as money transmitting businesses if
humans are running them to transfer funds.
These cases illustrate that if an MSB is willfully non-compliant, it risks not just fines but arrests. The FBI
and IRS-CI have actively pursued MSBs facilitating things like online fraud payments, drug money transfers,
or unregistered crypto ATMs.
Montana’s Context: A Montana-only MSB (serving local customers) might feel far from Washington, DC,
but the federal law reaches it. If such an MSB were to, say, handle proceeds of a scam (even unknowingly)
and had no AML controls, it could become entangled in a DOJ case. The best protection is rigorous
compliance: register, implement AML programs, file SARs so that you have reported any suspicious activity
(which can protect you from being viewed as willfully blind), and don’t operate in other states without
those licenses. DOJ can also use 18 U.S.C. § 1956 (money laundering) if an MSB is actively involved in
moving criminal money; that carries up to 20 years in prison, so one can see the stakes.

of Montana has the same federal obligations and will be judged by the same standards as an MSB in New

Compliance Officer: Designate an AML/BSA Compliance Officer responsible for program . Check:

Employee Training: Provide ongoing training on BSA/AML duties . Check: Training logs or
