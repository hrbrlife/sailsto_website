Series LLCs and Money Services Business (MSB)
Licensing: Legal Analysis
Introduction
A Series Limited Liability Company (Series LLC) is a unique business structure where a single “master”
LLC contains multiple series, each of which can hold assets, incur liabilities, and have members or
managers distinct from other series. The intent is to provide internal liability shields: the debts of one series
are enforceable only against that series’ assets, not against the master LLC or other series . Series
LLC statutes now exist in many jurisdictions (Delaware pioneered them in 1996, followed by states like
Illinois, Nevada, Texas, Wyoming, etc.), and some states have recently adopted the Uniform Protected Series
Act model (e.g. Florida, effective 2026) .
Money Services Businesses (MSBs) – including money transmitters, currency exchangers, check cashers,
issuers of payment instruments, etc. – are heavily regulated at both the federal and state levels. MSBs must
register with FinCEN (the U.S. Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network) under the Bank Secrecy
Act, and most states require MSBs (often specifically money transmitters) to be licensed. These regulations
generally apply to a “person” or “entity” engaging in money services. The intersection of Series LLCs with
MSB regulation raises complex questions: Is each series treated as a separate legal person that must
register and obtain licenses individually, or can the master LLC’s registration/license cover all series? How
are liability and compliance obligations allocated among series? Below, we address these issues in detail,
emphasizing primary legal sources and guidance.
1. Federal MSB Registration (FinCEN) and Series LLCs
FinCEN Registration Requirements: Under federal law (31 U.S.C. §5330 and FinCEN’s regulations), any
business that meets the definition of an MSB must register with FinCEN by filing the Registration of Money
Services Business (RMSB) – FinCEN Form 107 . This registration must be renewed every two years.
FinCEN’s rule at 31 C.F.R. §1022.380 (formerly 31 C.F.R. §103.41) lays out the MSB registration requirement.
All MSBs are required to register with FinCEN (whether or not also state-licensed) unless an exemption applies .
The primary exemption is for entities that are MSBs solely as agents of another MSB – such agents do not
register independently .
“One MSB, One Form” Principle: FinCEN instructs that a single business should file one registration form,
even if it has multiple owners, branches, or services . FinCEN’s guidance states: “Only one
registration form is required to be filed for each MSB… one form is required regardless of the manner in
which the MSB is structured (sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation or other structure)” .
Likewise, one form covers all branch locations of that MSB (the form asks for the number of branch offices)
. This implies that if FinCEN views a Series LLC’s series as part of one business “structure,” a single Form
107 could suffice. However, crucially, FinCEN has not explicitly addressed Series LLCs in its MSB guidance
to date. Recent FinCEN rules (e.g. the Corporate Transparency Act’s BOI reporting) likewise left open

Series as Separate MSBs or Branches: The crux is whether each series in a Series LLC is considered a
separate MSB “person” for FinCEN purposes. FinCEN regulations define “person” broadly to include entities,
but do not mention series. Under state laws like Delaware’s, a series can function like a distinct entity (it
may contract, hold property, sue/be sued in its own name if the LLC agreement allows) . Delaware
even defines “person” to include a “series thereof” . If each series is a separate legal person, each series
that engages in MSB activities would independently fall under the FinCEN registration mandate. In
that case, each series would need to register within 180 days of beginning MSB operations and obtain its
own FinCEN registration number. FinCEN’s RMSB form requires the MSB’s identifying information (including
EIN). Practically, a series LLC that treats each series as a separate MSB would obtain separate EINs for each
series (especially if they have separate tax filings or employees) and file separate Form 107s for each
series.
On the other hand, FinCEN could take the position that the master LLC is the MSB and the series are
merely internal divisions or “branches” of that MSB. FinCEN’s reference to “one form regardless of…
branches” might support this approach. In that scenario, the master LLC registers once as an MSB,
listing the locations of each series as branch offices or the series names as trade names. However, treating
series as branches is legally debatable because series (at least in some states) are not just branch offices –
they can be viewed as independently organized sub-companies. Notably, FinCEN has never issued
formal guidance or rulings explicitly permitting a Series LLC to file a single combined MSB
registration covering all series. In fact, legal commentators observe that FinCEN has not provided
specific guidance on series LLCs in the MSB context .
Implications of FinCEN’s Agent Exemption: FinCEN’s regulations do clarify that an MSB agent of another
MSB is not required to register . This is important for Series LLCs – it suggests a potential structuring
solution. If one series (say Series A) is the principal MSB (e.g., it operates a money transmission business
under the master LLC’s umbrella) and another series (Series B) provides MSB services solely as an agent of
Series A, then Series B would not need a separate FinCEN registration. In effect, Series A could register with
FinCEN and list the other series as its “agents.” For example, a Delaware Series LLC might appoint each
series that is customer-facing as an authorized delegate/agent of a lead series that holds the licenses and
registrations. FinCEN’s rule explicitly exempts businesses acting as authorized agents for a registered
MSB . Thus, if the inter-series relationships are structured as principal/agent, only the principal series
would register.
However, this approach requires that the agent-series not engage in MSB activity on its “own behalf” .
They would need formal agency agreements and operational control by the principal series, to fit FinCEN’s
definition of an agent (selling or distributing MSB services under the principal’s auspices). While legally
feasible, this arrangement might blur the independence of series and could draw scrutiny if not done
carefully.
Analysis: Absent direct FinCEN guidance, a conservative approach is to assume each series that
independently conducts MSB business should register separately with FinCEN (especially if each series
has distinct ownership or management). This avoids any risk of non-compliance if regulators deem each
series to be a separate “money transmitting business.” It also aligns with the IRS’s approach of treating each
series as a separate entity for tax purposes (discussed below), reinforcing the separateness of each series.

2. State Money Transmitter Licensing and Series LLCs
Most U.S. states (and D.C., P.R., etc.) regulate money transmitters (and often other MSB categories)
through licensing statutes. These laws generally require any “person” engaging in money transmission
(receiving money for transmission, selling payment instruments, etc.) to obtain a state license (unless
exempt, e.g. banks). Key question: does a Series LLC’s series count as a separate “person” that must be
licensed individually, or can one license for the master LLC cover the activities of all series?
General Variations Among States
State treatment of Series LLCs varies widely. Broadly:
States That Recognize Series as Separate Legal Entities: In a few jurisdictions, the law explicitly
allows a foreign or domestic series to be recognized and even independently registered for
business. These states often require or permit each series to obtain its own authority to do
business and, by extension, its own license if it engages in licensed activity. For example, Illinois,
which adopted series LLCs early, has a process to separately register each series of a foreign LLC that
will do business in IL, via a “certificate of designation” for each series . Illinois’s Money
Transmitters Act (205 ILCS 657/) does not explicitly mention series, but Illinois treats each series as
a “separate LLC” for most purposes. It is therefore likely that Illinois would require each series
transmitting money to be licensed in its own name, just as if each were a standalone company.
Indeed, Illinois regulators have emphasized that failing to separately maintain and register each
series can undermine the liability protections . Other states that allow individual series
registration include Indiana, Iowa, Nebraska, Arkansas, South Dakota . In those states, one
series of a foreign LLC can get a certificate of authority to do business (and potentially obtain a
license) even if the master LLC itself is not registered in that state (some allow a series to register
without the master) . If a state’s policy is to treat a series as an independent foreign “entity” for
qualification, it follows that the series must separately apply for any required license when
engaging in regulated business.
States That Do Not Individually Recognize Series (License the Master LLC Only): Many states,
including those that do not have domestic series LLC statutes, have no mechanism to register or
license an individual series apart from its master LLC. In these states, regulators typically deal only

States That Allow Series LLCs but License via Master (Silent or Implicit Policy): Some states do
have Series LLC statutes but still do not provide for separate foreign qualification of individual series.
These include, for example, Nevada, Kansas, North Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Ohio, Wyoming
among others . In these states, when a foreign Series LLC registers, it typically does so only as
a whole. There may be a requirement to list the names of the series on the foreign registration
application (e.g., Wyoming and Montana ask for series names to be listed) . But there is no
distinct certificate issued for each series. In such jurisdictions, regulators generally issue one license
to the applicant LLC and might attach an addendum or understanding that the LLC may operate via
its series. For example, Nevada allows series LLCs (domestic and foreign) but does not have a
mechanism for a series to individually register or be the named licensee for a state permit. We can
infer that Nevada’s Financial Institutions Division would require the master LLC to be the
licensee, covering all money transmission conducted by any series. Wyoming, which adopted series
LLCs in 2018, similarly would license the LLC itself. (Wyoming’s statutes allow listing series on a
foreign application, but do not treat them as separate legal entities for purposes of standing to hold
a state license.) In Texas (discussed below), the law until recently did not allow series to be
independently filed, which likely meant the Texas Department of Banking issues one license to the
series LLC as a whole.
States Issuing Series-Specific Guidance: A few states have explicitly pondered series in regulatory
context. For instance, Florida (which historically had no series LLCs) anticipated foreign series issues:
Florida law has long provided that each series of a foreign LLC may need to file a separate
application for authority in Florida . Now, Florida has passed a new Protected Series LLC law
(effective July 1, 2026) aligning with the uniform act . Under this law, each series (called a
“protected series”) will be treated much like a separate LLC, even though not a separate juridical
entity . The Florida statute explicitly says a series can sue and be sued in its own name and
will be considered a separate “person” under certain laws (e.g. UCC) . We can expect that by
2026, Florida’s Office of Financial Regulation will require each series that wants to engage in
money transmission to obtain its own license, or at least to register that series under the master’s

In summary, some states require each series to be licensed, others allow one license for the master

Focus Jurisdictions:
Montana: Montana is unique: it is one of the ten states that authorize Series LLCs (since 2013) ,
but Montana does not regulate money transmitters at all. The Montana Division of Banking
explicitly states: “Montana does not regulate money transmitters” . No state license is required
for MSB activities limited to Montana. Thus, a Series LLC organized or operating in Montana would
not need a Montana MTL. (Montana companies still must register with FinCEN and comply with
federal law, and if they do business in other states, those states’ licenses would be needed.) One
quirk: Montana does require when a foreign Series LLC registers in Montana that the operating
agreement for each series be attached – an unusual transparency requirement. This suggests
Montana recognizes series structure when a foreign LLC qualifies, but since no license is needed,
there is no licensing policy to navigate in-state. If a Montana Series LLC expands to licensed states, it
will face those states’ rules.
Delaware: Delaware is a leading Series LLC state. Under Delaware law, a series can function
independently (it can hold assets, have members, etc.) and Delaware explicitly allows “protected
series” and “registered series.” A registered series in Delaware obtains a certificate of registered
series from the Secretary of State and is somewhat closer to an independent entity (e.g., it can get a
certificate of good standing) . Delaware’s money transmitter law (5 Del. Code §2301 et seq.)
requires a license for any “person” engaging in selling or issuing payment instruments or
transmitting money . The statute defines “person” as an individual, partnership, association, joint-
stock association, or corporation . Notably, it does not explicitly list LLCs or series (likely an older
statute), but an LLC would fall under “association” or be treated as a corporation analog. Delaware’s
LLC Act defines a series as an “entity (or series thereof)” for some purposes , but it’s unclear if the
banking commissioner interprets “person” to include series. No published Delaware guidance
specifically addresses Series LLC licensing. In practice, if a Delaware Series LLC applies for a
Delaware money transmitter license, it would likely list the legal name of the licensee. If the
intention is to license the whole LLC (all series), the applicant name might just be “ABC LLC” without
reference to series, and perhaps the business plan would disclose that it operates through series.
Alternatively, a particular series could apply: e.g., “ABC LLC, Series 1”. Delaware might accept that
since a series can be a “person” under Delaware law . The safer approach within Delaware is to
license the master LLC and treat each series location as a branch or authorized delegate under that
license. There is no indication Delaware issues multiple transmitter licenses to different series of one
LLC; typically, one license covers the entity and all locations. Thus, Delaware likely allows one
license for the Series LLC as a whole, but counsel would disclose the series structure to the
regulator and perhaps get confirmation. (As an aside, if series are conducting substantially different
MSB businesses, Delaware might even suggest separate licenses to delineate activities, though that’s
speculative.)

Nevada: Nevada permits Series LLCs and has many crypto/MSB businesses. Nevada’s Money
Transmitter Act (NRS 671) requires licensure of persons engaging in money transmission. Nevada’s
corporate law allows series within LLCs, but the state has no separate foreign series filing (similar
to DE/TX prior to recent changes). The Nevada Financial Institutions Division has not published any
series-specific rule. It is reasonable to assume Nevada would license the LLC as one “person”. The
application (submitted on the NMLS system typically) would use the master LLC’s name and EIN. If a
series has a different name, the company might list it as a d/b/a or trade name in the application.
(Some state license applications ask for trade names under which the business will operate – series
could be listed there if they use distinct names.) So in Nevada, effectively one license covers the MSB
activity of all series, because the law does not contemplate issuing separate licenses to a non-entity
series.
Wyoming: Wyoming adopted Series LLCs in 2018 (Wyo. Stat. §17-29-2101 et seq.). Wyoming also is
known for crypto-friendly laws and has a Money Transmitter License exemption for certain digital
asset activities. Wyoming’s money transmitter statute (W.S. 40-22-101 et seq.) licenses any
“person” in the business of money transmission. Wyoming allows a foreign series LLC to register by
listing its series on the application . Wyoming’s Secretary of State form for foreign LLCs has a
section for series names. However, there is no provision for a series to register alone without the
master LLC. Accordingly, Wyoming’s Division of Banking would license the master LLC. They may ask
which series will be conducting the business, but they likely view the series as branch offices or
trade styles of the licensee. The licensee on record remains the master LLC. One practical
consideration: bonding and examination. Wyoming requires a minimum net worth and bonding for
licensees. If the series LLC’s assets are partitioned, the “licensee” (master LLC) might technically have
limited assets of its own, so meeting net worth might require looking at consolidated finances or
obtaining guarantees. Regulators could demand that all series jointly satisfy financial
requirements or even require an undertaking that assets of other series won’t be off-limits for
obligations of the licensed series. (No explicit rule on that, but a risk we consider in the liability
section.)
Illinois: Illinois was an early adopter of Series LLCs (805 ILCS 180/37-40). In Illinois, each series can,
through a Certificate of Designation, be treated as a separate entity for certain purposes (it gets a
unique name and the Secretary of State maintains a listing of active series). Illinois explicitly permits
a foreign series to get a certificate of authority separate from the LLC . Therefore, if a Delaware
Series LLC wanted to conduct licensed business in Illinois, it could register the specific series that will
do the business (rather than the entire LLC). The Illinois Department of Financial and Professional
Regulation (IDFPR), which oversees money transmitters, has not published formal guidance on
Series LLC licensing, but given the statutory framework, each series engaging in money
transmission should obtain its own Illinois Transmitter License. An Illinois regulator would likely
treat a series like any other applicant – for example, “XYZ LLC Series A” could be the named licensee,
with its own bond and examination, separate from “XYZ LLC Series B”. Indeed, Illinois emphasizes
corporate separateness: “A Series LLC allows for creating separate series … with their own interests,
assets, and liabilities” , and caution that failing to properly separate accounts or co-mingling
funds could negate the liability shield . This indicates Illinois expects series to behave like
distinct companies, which would include individually complying with licensing.
Texas: Texas has allowed Series LLCs since 2009 (Tex. Bus. Orgs. Code §101.601 et seq.), and
significantly updated the law in 2022. Under Texas law, a series is not a separate domestic entity –

LLC. The flipside is that the TxDOB will consider the consolidated financials of the whole LLC when

California, New York, Florida (and others): As discussed, California and New York do not yet have
series LLC statutes, and do not provide for recognizing foreign series independently. They will treat a
series LLC like any other LLC – one license per LLC. California’s DFPI, for example, will issue a license
to “Acme LLC.” If Acme LLC happens to internally segregate operations into Series 1, 2, 3, that is
invisible to the DFPI. From a compliance standpoint, this raises an issue: if Series 1 (the crypto
transmission division) is licensed and following all rules, but Series 2 (another division) is engaged in
unlicensed activity out-of-state, the regulator could still view Acme LLC as the licensee
responsible for any money transmission it or its divisions conduct anywhere. The company
must ensure all series comply, since a violation by one series could jeopardize the single license for
the LLC. New York DFS, similarly, would hold the LLC accountable for all of its money transmission
business (and New York’s license, once granted, covers transmission nationwide for enforcement
purposes, not just in NY). As for Florida, by 2026 series will be recognized; it’s likely Florida will
require a separate license for each series or at least mandate that each protected series be
specifically authorized under a license. The new Florida series law treats each series “as if it were a
separate LLC” for most purposes , including being a separate “person” that can incur liabilities
. So a Florida Money Transmitter license could conceivably be issued to “XYZ LLC, Series Alpha” as
a licensee distinct from Series Beta of the same LLC. Until the law takes effect and guidance is issued,
we anticipate a case-by-case approach in Florida – possibly requiring the master LLC to hold an
“umbrella” license and also listing each series that will operate, or requiring each series to apply
(Florida’s OFR might provide forms for a “registered foreign series” to be licensed under its own
name once the Dept. of State can register them).

As the chart indicates, Illinois, Nebraska (and a few others) stand out as jurisdictions likely to demand

license only the master LLC in certain states, while in others it might have to license a particular series
(or multiple series) separately. This creates complexity in compliance (maintaining multiple licenses within
one overall enterprise). In some cases, reorganizing into separate traditional LLCs could even be simpler
from a licensing perspective – a point to consider in risk analysis.
3. Liability Isolation and Regulatory Implications in MSB Context
One of the principal advantages of a Series LLC is the internal liability shield: liabilities of one series
should not spill over to the assets of the master or sister series, provided statutory requirements (separate
records, etc.) are met. However, when an MSB series faces financial failure or regulatory enforcement, will
that shield truly hold up? Regulators and courts are still testing these issues, especially in states that don’t
have series legislation.
Status of Liability Shields: Under Delaware law (and similarly in Illinois, Nevada, Texas, etc.), if a series LLC
maintains separateness (e.g. separate books and accounts for each series), then creditors of one series
have no claim on the assets of other series or the LLC itself (and vice versa) . This “horizontal”
liability protection is codified in statutes (Del. Code tit. 6, §18-215(b); Texas Bus. Orgs. Code §101.609, etc.).
But what happens in practice if, say, Series X (which conducted money transmission) becomes insolvent
and cannot meet customer obligations or regulatory fines? Normally, only Series X’s assets would be
available to claimants. Other series and the parent LLC should be insulated.

Regulatory Perspective: State regulators issue licenses to ensure consumer funds are protected (often
requiring surety bonds, minimum net worth, permissible investments, etc.). If a licensed series were to fail
financially, the regulator’s concern is consumer restitution. Legally, the regulator (or consumers) might
attempt to pierce the series veil or argue the series is not truly separate, particularly in a state that doesn’t
recognize series. For example, in a non-series state, a court might find that the concept of series isolation is
against that state’s public policy if it leaves consumers uncompensated. There is scant case law directly on
this point, but some analogies exist:
In Alphonse v. Arch Bay Holdings, L.L.C., a 2013 case in federal court, a borrower sued the wrong
party in a foreclosure – he sued the master LLC (Arch Bay Holdings), whereas the note was actually
held by a specific series of that LLC. The U.S. Fifth Circuit noted that the “separate juridical status” of
a series, especially in a state (Louisiana) with no series law, was an open question and remanded for
fact-finding . The court did not flatly ignore the series structure; instead it asked whether the
series and LLC had “identical interests” or were in fact distinct . This suggests that if the series
was run distinctly (separate records, etc.), the court might respect the shield, but if not, it might treat
the LLC and series as one. Ultimately, Arch Bay’s series was found to be the proper party, indicating
the liability shield held – the parent LLC was not liable for the series’ debt (the mortgage) . This
case, often cited, gives some comfort that courts may uphold the series shield even in foreign
forums, provided formalities are observed. But it also warns that insufficient separation (identical
interests) could undermine it.
Another context is bankruptcy: If a series MSB went bankrupt, can it file for bankruptcy separately
from the LLC? There is debate; some scholars argue a series should be an eligible debtor if treated as
a separate entity, but the Bankruptcy Code doesn’t explicitly recognize series. A series might be
treated as an “unincorporated association” for eligibility. If one series files bankruptcy, there’s a risk a
court could substantively consolidate the assets of the whole LLC if separateness isn’t clear. While not
directly a licensing matter, it affects liability isolation for creditors.
Enforcement actions: Suppose a regulator fines Series A $1 million for compliance failures. If Series
A doesn’t have funds, can the regulator enforce against the LLC or other series? The answer may
depend on state law. In states like Delaware, the statute would say no – only Series A’s assets are
liable . But a regulator might argue the license was held by the LLC (if that’s the case), so the
LLC as licensee is responsible for payment, thus effectively bypassing the internal shield. Indeed, if
only the master LLC’s name appears on the license certificate, the agency might treat any fine or
penalty as a debt of the licensee (the master). This is a crucial nuance: when only the master LLC is
the recognized licensee (as in most states), liability for regulatory penalties or bond claims could
attach to the master LLC notwithstanding the series structure. In contrast, if a series itself is the
named licensee (as could happen in IL or other series-recognizing states), then only that series is on
the hook.
Reach of Assets of Non-Transmitting Series: If one series fails financially (e.g., cannot repay customer
funds), will regulators or courts leave other series’ assets untouched? They should, if the law of the series’
formation state applies and the company respected formalities. However, imagine a scenario: Series M
(money transmitter) in a Delaware LLC suffers a huge loss and consumers in a non-series state sue. They
might sue the master LLC and Series M jointly, claiming the series is just an alter ego. If the series kept
wholly separate books, accounts, and the LLC agreement clearly delineates separation, the defense would
invoke Delaware law to uphold the shield. Given the paucity of direct precedent, there is a risk that a court

Bonding and Net Worth Requirements: State MSB licenses often require a surety bond (to protect
customers if the licensee fails) and minimum net worth or permissible investments equal to outstanding
transmission liabilities. How are these calculated in a Series LLC? If each series is separately licensed, each
series must post its own bond and meet net worth requirements individually. For example, if Illinois
requires a $100,000 bond per licensee, and a Series LLC has three series each licensed in IL, that could
mean three bonds of $100k each, not one bond covering all. Likewise, net worth (often defined as equity or
assets minus liabilities) would be assessed per series – which might be problematic if the series is thinly
capitalized relying on the broader LLC’s strength. Regulators could insist that each licensed series maintain
the required net worth on a stand-alone basis or perhaps require a guarantee from the parent LLC or
another series. If instead a single license covers the whole LLC, then one bond is posted in the name of the
LLC (covering all series operations in aggregate). This is simpler administratively, but it means all series are
effectively backing that bond via the master LLC. A claim on the bond due to one series’s failure could
indirectly impact the assets of other series if the surety seeks reimbursement from the LLC (the surety’s
contract is with the LLC). Thus, the liability shield might not protect against bond recourse, because the
bond likely binds the entire LLC. In states where each series is licensed and bonded separately, a bond claim
on Series A’s bond should not involve Series B at all (assuming no cross-guarantee).
Cross-Series Guarantees and Capital Flow: In practice, MSB regulators care about keeping customer
funds safe. If one series is struggling, a regulator might ask the master LLC to prop it up or transfer capital
from another series. Doing so could blur the lines of separateness (commingling funds across series),
potentially jeopardizing the liability protection in the eyes of a court . It’s a delicate balance: you want to
maintain strict separation to preserve the liability shield, yet from a regulatory risk standpoint, you may
want the flexibility to support a troubled series to prevent consumer harm. A formal approach could be to
have the master LLC (or a non-MSB series) act as a contractual guarantor for obligations of the licensed
series. That would give regulators comfort but note: if the master guarantees the series’s obligations, a
creditor could enforce that guarantee against the master LLC’s assets (which could include other series’
interests). Essentially it voluntarily links the liability. Some MSB firms might do this intentionally (e.g., to
satisfy a state’s net worth requirements if the series alone doesn’t have enough capital, the parent LLC
provides a guarantee or letter of credit).
Enforcement Case Study: While not specifically about a series LLC, consider a hypothetical enforcement:
Series X (licensed in State A) fails to file transaction reports and is fined $500k. If Series X doesn’t pay, State
A might revoke the license. If Series X has no assets to pay restitution to customers, the state might try to
claim the bond (if any) and possibly pursue the parent LLC for any shortfall if the parent was the licensee or
guarantor. If parent LLC wasn’t licensee, the state might not have privity to fine the parent, but could, for
example, refuse to license any other series or the parent in the future (collateral consequences). In multi-
state operations, a blow-up in one series could trigger reputational and cross-jurisdictional issues
affecting the whole LLC.
Summary of Risk to Liability Shield: The series liability shield likely holds in most circumstances if
formalities are respected, even when one series engages in risky MSB activity and others do not. Delaware

4. Practical Structuring Considerations for Series LLC MSBs
Using a Series LLC for an MSB can offer efficiencies (single parent entity, consolidated administration) but
also poses unique compliance challenges. Here are best practices and structural recommendations to
maintain series separation and satisfy regulators:
Clearly Document Series Separateness in the Operating Agreement: The LLC Operating
Agreement should explicitly provide that each series is a separate “cell” with its own assets,
liabilities, business purpose, and records . Include provisions that each series shall be solely
responsible for its debts and that members associated with one series have no rights to assets of
another series. Specify that Series A is intended to operate the money transmission business,
and for example, Series B operates a separate line (or holds passive assets) and will not commingle
funds with Series A. Incorporate any statutory language required by the series LLC law (e.g.,
Delaware requires notice in the certificate or agreement of the limitation of liabilities). The O/A can
also outline inter-series relationships: if one series will act as agent for another, detail that agency in
writing to reinforce the roles for FinCEN exemption purposes.
Separate EINs, Bank Accounts, and Books: Treat each series much like an independent company
internally. Obtain a separate EIN (Employer ID Number) for each series that will have employees,
bank accounts, or tax filings . Open distinct bank accounts in the name of each series (e.g., “XYZ
LLC – Series A Account”) and never co-mingle funds between series accounts . Maintain a distinct
general ledger for each series, tracking its assets, liabilities, income, and expenses. Prepare
separate financial statements for each series (even if you also combine them for overall analysis).
This level of rigor not only preserves the liability shield (showing creditors and courts that records
are “maintained contemporaneously and separately” as required ) but also will likely be
expected by state examiners. If an MSB regulator audits “Series A,” you should be able to produce
records solely of Series A’s business. Wyoming’s statute, for example, demands that records
“identify the assets of each series” with specificity – a good rule of thumb everywhere.
Distinct Identities in Business Operations: Ensure that each series holds itself out as a separate
unit to the extent practical. If Series A is customer-facing, use a distinct name (many states require
the series name to include the master LLC’s name, but you can still distinguish, e.g. “XYZ Money
Services LLC – Series Alpha”). Use that name on contracts, receipts, websites, and advertisements
when referring to the MSB services of that series. This avoids confusion and further buttresses that
customers and partners are dealing with Series A (not the entire LLC or another series). Some banks
and payment processors may not be familiar with series – be prepared to show them the certificate
of formation and operating agreement to prove Series A’s authority to open accounts or enter
contracts. File any necessary assumed name certificates if a series name doesn’t include the LLC
name (Texas, e.g., requires the LLC to file an assumed name for any protected series operating

licenses should apply, others should refrain from regulated activity to avoid inadvertently

In States Where Only the Master LLC Can Be Licensed, Use Internal Agency Agreements: If you

series structure, draft an internal agency or services agreement between the master LLC and the
series that operate the MSB business. For instance, “Series A will conduct money transmission on
behalf of [Master] LLC in X state, and LLC hereby appoints Series A as its agent to carry out licensed
activities.” This way, it’s documented that Series A’s actions are done under the authority of the
licensed entity. It also could support the argument that Series A need not separately register with
FinCEN (since it’s an agent of the master LLC MSB) . Such agreements also allocate fees,
responsibilities, compliance duties, etc. between the master and series. Ensure this doesn’t conflict
with any prohibition in state law about subdelegation – but generally authorized delegates of an
MSB can be any business, including possibly an affiliate.
Implement Compliance Programs per Series or Consolidated with Clear Attribution: MSBs must
have AML/BSA compliance programs, bank accounts for holding customer funds, cybersecurity
measures, etc. Decide whether each series will have its own compliance program and officer or if
there will be one program at the LLC level. Both approaches can work, but regulators will want to see
that nothing “falls through the cracks.” If one AML program covers multiple series, it should explicitly
list the distinct products or business lines (by series) and ensure risk assessments consider each
series’ activities. Alternatively, if Series A is the only MSB, then the compliance program can be
scoped to Series A only. All reports (e.g. SARs, CTRs) should use the correct entity information.
FinCEN’s systems (for SAR filing) allow inclusion of multiple DBA names, etc., so if the master LLC is
the filer but the series is branded differently, include that. Training should cover personnel of each
series relevant to their activities. Essentially, treat each series with the seriousness of an
independent MSB in terms of compliance culture. This also helps if one series gets in trouble – you
can demonstrate that the issue was isolated to that series and not systemic across the whole LLC.
Avoid Commingling Assets and Liabilities: This is vital. Do not use one series’ funds to pay another
series’ expenses (except through explicit, arms-length inter-series loans or agreements documented
in writing). If resources must be shared, do it by formal agreement: e.g., a Shared Services
Agreement where the master LLC or a management series provides services to all series for a fee,
or inter-company loans with promissory notes. Always document any asset transfer between series
and carry it out at fair value. For instance, if Series B (unlicensed) owns office equipment that Series
A (licensed) uses, have Series A lease it or pay rent to Series B. Paperwork like this may feel

cost (in theory). These provisions can reassure members or managers that if one series’s MSB
venture fails, it won’t drag down the rest.
Capitalization and Liquidity Planning: Ensure each MSB-operating series is adequately capitalized
on its own. Regulators will examine the financial statements. If Series A is the transmitter, ideally it
should hold the cash reserves and capital needed to meet bonding and customer obligations, not
rely on inter-series support. If multiple series are licensed in different states, avoid a situation where,
say, Series A holds all the money but Series B has the license elsewhere but no assets. That could
lead to non-compliance with capital requirements and complicate the regulator’s ability to assess
safety. If capital must be pooled for efficiency, consider having the master LLC hold common
capital and then formally contribute it to series or guarantee series obligations. But note that
contributions should be clearly recorded in each series’ books.
Choosing Which Series Conducts MSB Activities: Some Series LLCs may only want one series (or a
few) to actually engage in MSB business, while other series hold investments or other businesses.
This is a good strategy to ring-fence risk. Designate a single series as the “MSB Series” and have all
money transmission operations run through it exclusively. Other series should have a covenant in
the operating agreement not to engage in MSB activities (so they don’t inadvertently trigger
licensing needs or BSA obligations). This way, only one series and the master need to deal with MSB
compliance. This isolated series can even be structured to be bankruptcy-remote if needed, without
entangling the others.
Insurance and Bonding: Obtain appropriate insurance (e.g., fidelity bond, cyber insurance, E&O
insurance) in the name of the series that is operating the MSB. If only the master LLC can be the
named insured (some insurers might not understand series), list the series as an additional insured
or clarify coverage applies to its activities. Similarly, surety bonds should ideally list the licensed
entity (series or LLC) and cover the specific obligations of that licensee. If one bond is covering
multiple states for one series, ensure it’s sized accordingly. Avoid using one series’ assets as
collateral for another series’ bond unless absolutely necessary (cross-collateralization could be seen
as inconsistent with separation , though not conclusively illegal).
Corporate Governance Formalities: Treat each series somewhat like a subsidiary: hold separate
unit meetings if required, or at least separate resolutions authorizing major actions for each series.
For example, if the Series LLC has a board or managers, when they approve a big contract or a policy
for Series A, minute it as a Series A decision. Issue separate membership interest certificates for each
series (if the LLC agreement calls for them) and track ownership separately. If new investors come in
for one series, document that clearly (many series LLCs use series to house different investor pools
for different projects – that should be clearly delineated). These measures all demonstrate respect
for the series’ individuality, which is crucial if a court later reviews the structure in a liability dispute.

Tax and Accounting Strategy: Decide how each series will be treated for tax. The IRS’s proposed

do that to isolate liabilities and tax obligations). Ensure that you’ve obtained any required IRS rulings

master by election, which is typically not applicable because the master LLC usually isn’t the owner of

same parent corporation, they could each be disregarded entities of that parent. But if the master
LLC itself has members, then each series might be a partnership among those members (which
could mean multiple K-1s, etc.). This can get complex, so involve a tax advisor. From a regulatory
view, having separate financial statements per series (as mentioned) helps show each series’s
financial integrity.
In sum, treating series with formal discipline akin to separate subsidiaries is the golden rule. It
preserves the liability shield and provides transparency for regulators. Many of these practices are more
onerous than running a series LLC informally, but regulators and courts will expect this level of rigor if you
want the advantages of series without the pitfalls.

considerations include obtaining EINs, classification of series for income tax, and reporting of income
(especially if different series have different activities or profits). Key points:
Entity Classification: In 2010, the IRS issued Proposed Regulations (§301.7701-1(a)(5)) providing
that each series of a domestic series LLC (or series trust, etc.) is treated as a separate entity for

corporation, depending on its own characteristics (number of owners, elections), irrespective of the
master LLC’s classification . Although those regulations were proposed and not immediately
finalized, the IRS has generally followed their approach. For instance, the IRS has issued private
letter rulings treating individual series as separate taxpayers (one PLR treated each cell of an
insurance series as a separate insurance company for tax) . The American Bar Association noted
the IRS position essentially “treats each series as a separate tax entity” . Implication: If your
Series LLC has multiple series conducting business (especially if they have different ownership or
operations like one is an MSB, another is an investment vehicle), expect to file separate tax returns
for each series (unless all members elect to aggregate them, which usually isn’t allowed across
series).
EINs: The IRS will issue an EIN to a series if you state that the series is a segment of a series LLC.
Many practitioners report no trouble getting EINs for each series (often you list the legal name as
“Series X of Y LLC”). The IRS even updated Form SS-4 instructions to accommodate series in some
cases. Each series that has employees, or that will file an information return (like a partnership
return) or its own tax return, must have its own EIN . If a series is disregarded (single
member, and not needed to file excise or employment taxes separately), it might use the owner’s EIN
– but since the owner might be the master LLC or individuals, that can complicate compliance. It’s
generally cleaner for each series to have an EIN for banking and compliance purposes. For example,
FinCEN’s MSB Registration form asks for the registrant’s EIN; if each series is registering, use each
series’s EIN.
Tax Reporting and Compliance: If Series A is an MSB handling cryptocurrency exchanges (for
example) and Series B is a passive investment series, their income and deductions must be
accounted for separately. Series A might have ordinary business income from fees, while Series B
has portfolio income. The IRS will not allow offset of one series’s losses against another’s gains
unless they are in the same tax entity (which by default they aren’t). Each series files its own Form
1065 (if partnership) or 1120 (if C-corp elected) or Schedule C (if disregarded by an individual owner).
Payroll taxes: If each series has employees, each should have its own payroll tax filings and deposits
linked to its EIN. For sales tax or other state taxes, treat each series as separate if required to
register (some states might allow consolidated sales tax for the LLC, but others might not recognize
the series).
Consolidated Financial Audit: Even though tax is separate, you might maintain an overall
consolidated financial view for management. But for regulatory net worth calculations and bonding,
use the appropriate series’s figures. Some states might ask for parent (master LLC) financials if they
license only the parent; in that case, be prepared to present both consolidated and series-level
statements to demonstrate the parent’s financial health without double-counting assets.

Each risk above is coupled with mitigation strategies to manage or reduce the risk. Overall, while a Series
LLC can provide internal liability segregation and administrative streamlining, it requires disciplined
management. The risk matrix underscores that regulatory ambiguity and operational complexity are the
highest concerns, but they can be addressed with proactive compliance and clear internal boundaries.
Recommended Best Practices & Structuring Guidance:
1. Concentrate MSB operations in a dedicated series – this simplifies licensing and compliance since only
that series (and the master as needed) deals with regulators. Keep other ventures in separate series to
firewall them.
2. Ensure each series’ independent existence through formalities: separate finances, contracts, EINs, and
21
clear public identities . This not only protects liability shields but also makes it easier to demonstrate
compliance (e.g., showing a regulator the specific series books).
3. Engage regulators with full disclosure – do not hide the series structure. In applications and renewal
reports, note the series LLC status and which series are involved. Many states appreciate transparency and
will often accommodate novel structures if you’ve shown good faith in explaining them.
4. Use the agent exemption smartly – if one series can be the “principal” MSB, make other series its
agents (with formal agreements) so they don’t each need federal registration . Just be cautious that
those agent series are truly operating under the principal’s supervision and BSA program, so the
arrangement is genuine.
5. Craft internal agreements for shared services and guarantees – if multiple series share resources (IT
systems, compliance staff) or if the master provides capital, document it with inter-company agreements.
This both legitimizes the transactions for auditors and avoids unintended dissolution of separateness.
6. Monitor evolving laws – The legal environment is changing (e.g., new series legislation in Florida ,
potential FinCEN rulings). Assign someone (or outside counsel) to keep track of developments, especially in
states where you operate. Be ready to adapt (for example, if a state suddenly requires each series to file
something or a new NMLS question arises about series, address it promptly).
7. Be prepared to restructure if necessary – Flexibility is key. If ultimately the series structure becomes a
hindrance (perhaps due to a recalcitrant state regulator or investor concerns), be ready with a plan to spin
off series into subsidiaries or merge series together. The Series LLC should not become an iron cage; it’s a
tool, and if the tool stops being useful, convert to a more accepted structure. Fortunately, many series
statutes have provisions for converting a series to a standalone LLC or merging series. For instance,
Delaware allows a protected series to be converted to an LLC (by filing certificates of conversion). Keep this
contingency in mind.
In conclusion, Series LLCs can be effectively utilized in the money services industry, but they demand
careful compliance management. The lack of one-size-fits-all regulatory treatment means the organization
must tailor its approach state-by-state and federally, maintaining the overarching principle of
separation. By following the best practices above, a Series LLC MSB can enjoy the benefits of internal
segregation while minimizing legal risk and satisfying regulators that each series is operating safely and
lawfully within its own lane.
