Agent-of-Payee Exemption from Money
Transmitter Licensing Requirements
Federal Treatment of Agent-of-Payee Arrangements
FinCEN’s Position: At the federal level, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) recognizes a
“payment processor” exemption that is functionally similar to the agent-of-payee concept. FinCEN’s
regulations define a “money transmitter” as a person that accepts funds from one person and transmits
them to another person or location . However, the rules also enumerate circumstances under which a
person’s activities do not make them a money transmitter. Notably, the acceptance and transmission of
funds integral to the sale of goods or provision of services (other than money transmission itself) will not
cause that person to be a money transmitter . This principle underpins the agent-of-payee concept: if
an intermediary is merely collecting payments on behalf of a seller of goods/services, the funds movement
is considered incidental to the underlying transaction and not a separate money transmission business.
2011 Final Rule and “Payment Processor” Exemption: In its 2011 MSB Final Rule, FinCEN codified an
exemption for certain payment processors acting as agents of merchants or creditors . FinCEN
explicitly acknowledged that while such payment processors technically move funds, the service is ancillary
to their primary business of facilitating commerce . The rule excludes from the “money transmitter”
definition any person who “acts as a payment processor to facilitate the purchase of, or payment of a bill for, a
good or service through a clearance and settlement system by [a BSA-regulated financial institution]” when
certain criteria are met . In short, if an intermediary processes payments on behalf of the payee
(seller/creditor), rather than on behalf of the payer, it may fall outside federal money transmission
requirements.
FinCEN Administrative Rulings: FinCEN rulings have further clarified the scope of this exemption. In a
2014 interpretive ruling, FinCEN outlined four conditions that must be satisfied for the “payment processor
exemption” (agent-of-payee) to apply :
Payment for Goods/Services: The service must facilitate the purchase of goods or services, or payment
of bills for goods/services (other than money transmission). In other words, the funds transfer is tied
to an underlying consumer transaction (e.g. paying a merchant or biller) .
Use of Regulated Financial Networks: The payments must be processed through clearing and
settlement systems that only admit BSA-regulated financial institutions (e.g. ACH networks,
card networks, or bank rails) . This ensures the intermediary isn’t operating an entirely
independent money transfer system; it’s piggybacking on the traditional banking/payment system.
Formal Agreement with Payee: The intermediary must provide the service pursuant to a formal
agreement with the payee (the seller or creditor) . A written contract should appoint the
intermediary as the payee’s agent for purposes of receiving payments.

Agent of the Payee (Not the Payer): The agreement must be at a minimum with the payee/creditor
who provided the goods or services (i.e. the entity to whom money is owed) . In practice this
means the intermediary is acting on behalf of the payee, and not on behalf of the customer. FinCEN
emphasizes that the intermediary should be contractually responsible to the payee, not the
consumer .
When these conditions are all met, FinCEN has ruled that the intermediary’s activities do not make it an MSB
money transmitter . FinCEN reasoned that under agency law principles, “receipt of funds by the agent is
tantamount to receipt by the payee”, so there is no point at which the intermediary is holding funds for
transmission to a third party . Instead, the funds are considered to have been paid to the
merchant/creditor at the moment the agent receives them, extinguishing the payer’s obligation.
Relation to 31 CFR §1010.100(ff)(5)(ii)(A)–(F): These provisions enumerate exclusions from the “money
transmitter” definition. Subsection (ff)(5)(ii) specifically carves out certain activities, including the payment
processor scenario. For example, subsection (B) excludes a person who acts as a payment processor for
goods or services via a clearance and settlement system as described above . Likewise, FinCEN has
identified other integral payment scenarios (such as certain billing services, payroll processors, or debt
management programs) that do not trigger money transmitter status . The agent-of-payee concept
falls squarely within these exclusions – it is effectively the federal counterpart of the state “agent-of-payee”
exemption. FinCEN’s commentary makes clear that if an entity’s funds transfer role is only a byproduct of its
service to merchants or other payees, and it complies with the conditions above, it is not treated as a money
transmitter .
It should be noted that this federal exemption is narrowly construed. FinCEN rulings (e.g. FIN-2003-08) have
emphasized that if an intermediary strays beyond the limited role of agent of the payee – for instance, by
acting on behalf of payers or handling funds outside of regulated clearing networks – the exemption would
not apply . But in general, FinCEN has embraced the principle that “agents of the payee” are not
independent transmitters, aligning the federal position with the agent-of-payee exemptions that many
states have adopted.
FinCEN Enforcement Actions: We are not aware of any FinCEN enforcement action that solely turns on an
agent-of-payee claim. FinCEN’s enforcement in the MSB space tends to target unregistered money
transmitters or BSA/AML violations. However, FinCEN’s published rulings and guidance imply that a
company improperly claiming to be an agent-of-payee (when it does not meet the criteria) would be viewed
as operating an unlicensed MSB. FinCEN’s 2019 guidance on money transmission explicitly warned that
labeling oneself a “payment processor” is not sufficient – the facts and contractual relationships must
support that the entity is an agent of the payee and not acting on behalf of the payer . In practice,
companies relying on the agent-of-payee concept at the federal level should be prepared to demonstrate
that they satisfy the four FinCEN criteria above. Failure to do so could result in FinCEN deeming the activity
unlicensed money transmission with attendant penalties.
State-by-State Analysis of Agent-of-Payee Exemptions
State money transmission laws have historically been quite strict, but in the past decade a majority of states
have embraced the agent-of-payee exemption in some form. As of 2025, 38 states (and counting) explicitly
recognize that a company receiving payments as an agent of a seller/payee is not required to hold a money
transmitter license . Several others permit it by regulatory interpretation or on a case-by-case basis.

Only a small minority of jurisdictions (roughly a dozen states and D.C.) do not recognize this exemption and
still treat such intermediaries as licensable transmitters . Below is a comprehensive state-by-state
overview, categorized as requested:
(a) States with Statutory Agent-of-Payee Exemptions: These states have enacted explicit provisions
(typically in their money transmission act) exempting transactions where the intermediary is an agent of the
payee. Such statutes generally require a written contract appointing the agent and specify that payment
to the agent is deemed payment to the payee. Many of these were adopted via recent money transmitter
modernization laws. Key examples:
California: California’s Money Transmission Act exempts “any transaction in which the recipient of
money is an agent of the payee, pursuant to a preexisting written contract, and delivery of the money to
the agent satisfies the payor’s obligation to the payee.” . In 2014, California added this exemption by
statute (Cal. Fin. Code §2010(l)), and in 2021 the DFPI finalized regulations clarifying that “receipt of
money by an agent of the payee from a payor is equivalent to receipt by the payee… The agent of a payee
has not received money for transmission.” . (The regulation also underscores that this doesn’t apply
to agents of the payor, only of the payee .) California’s exemption requires that the payee is
providing goods or services (other than money transmission) and that the agency agreement
explicitly extinguishes the payer’s liability upon payment to the agent . California has also
clarified that the exemption does not apply to stored-value or prepaid card loads; those remain
regulated (by separate regulation, 10 C.C.R. §80.126.30).
Georgia: Georgia Code §7-1-682(a)(12) (amended 2023) provides that a license is not required for “[a]
person appointed as an agent of a payee to collect and process a payment from a payor to the payee for
goods or services, other than money transmission itself, provided that” three conditions are met :
(A) There is a written agreement between payee and agent directing the agent to collect payments
on the payee’s behalf; (B) The payee is held out to the public as accepting payments via that agent;
and (C) Payment to the agent is treated as received by the payee, extinguishing the payor’s
obligation and “there is no risk of loss to the payor if the agent fails to remit.” . Georgia added this
exemption in 2016 (with further updates in 2023) . The requirement that the payee hold the
agent out to the public and that the payor bear no risk are common threads in many states’ laws.
Illinois: Illinois did not have a statutory agent-of-payee exemption until very recently. Historically,
the Illinois Division of Financial Institutions acknowledged via guidance that certain payment
processors acting as agents of merchants were not required to be licensed . In 2020, Illinois issued
a formal “non-binding statement” reaffirming that a company facilitating ACH payments from
customers to merchants under an agency agreement did not need an Illinois Transmitter of Money
Act (TOMA) license . As of 2024, Illinois has moved to codify this: a new Illinois money
transmission modernization bill (SB 3412 in the 103rd General Assembly) includes an agent-of-payee
exemption similar to the CSBS Model Law (this bill was pending or recently enacted as of late 2024)
. Update: Illinois’ Act 103-991 (signed in 2023) modernizes the TOMA effective January 1,
2025, and it expressly adds that an agent appointed by a payee to collect payments on the payee’s
behalf for goods or services is exempt (with criteria mirroring the model law). This aligns Illinois with
the majority position.
Texas: Texas historically relied on common-law agency principles rather than a statute. In a 2014
policy memorandum, the Texas Department of Banking stated that an agent collecting payments on

Washington: Washington State explicitly added an agent-of-payee exemption in a 2017 amendment
to its Uniform Money Services Act. RCW 19.230.020(9)(c) excludes from licensing any person “that
facilitates payment for goods or services (not including money transmission itself) or bill payment…
pursuant to a written contract with the payee,” so long as either (i) payment to the processor satisfies
the payor’s obligation to the payee or (ii) that obligation is otherwise extinguished . Washington’s
law uniquely also requires that the payment processing occur through a “clearance and settlement
process using BSA-regulated institutions.” In effect, Washington built FinCEN’s condition into its
statute. The “payee” is defined as the provider of goods or services owed the funds. Washington’s
exemption is widely relied on by payment intermediaries in e-commerce and bill-pay. (Notably,
Washington formerly had a policy of issuing license waivers to payment processors case-by-case, but
the statutory exemption obviated the need for individual waivers .)
New Jersey: New Jersey adopted an agent-of-payee statutory exemption in 2022 as part of its Money
Transmitter Modernization Act (effective 2023). The NJ law (N.J. Stat. §17:15C-3, as amended) closely
tracks the model language: an agent collecting payments on behalf of a merchant pursuant to a
written agreement is not deemed to be transmitting money. (Note: New Jersey was previously one of
the states without the exemption, but the recent legislation brought it on board. This change post-
dates some sources that list NJ as not having the exemption.)
Other statutory-adopting states: Over 25 states now have similar language on the books. This
includes Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky,
Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, Nevada, North Carolina, Ohio,
Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin, and
others, many of which enacted or updated their laws in 2021–2024 to incorporate the “agent of a
payee” clause . For example, Colorado added an agent-of-payee exemption in 2018; Ohio did
so in its 2022 money transmitter act overhaul; Virginia included one in a 2018 amendment; Nevada
in 2021; Hawaii in 2022 as it ended its sandbox program. Each of these generally requires the
familiar elements (written agency contract and payment considered made to the payee upon receipt
by agent).
It is worth noting that while the core concept is uniform, some state statutes have minor variations. A few
impose additional nuances – e.g. Nevada specifies that the payee must be “liable to the agent for amounts
received” (implying the agent assumes responsibility to remit); Tennessee’s exemption is limited to agents of
merchants selling goods or services (explicitly excluding agents of a payee that is itself a money
transmitter). Overall, however, the statutory formulations are very similar, thanks in large part to the
influence of the CSBS Model Money Transmission Modernization Act, which contains an agent-of-payee

(b) States Recognizing the Exemption via Regulation or Guidance: A handful of states do not have an
explicit statute, but the state regulator has publicly taken the position (through regulations, opinions, or no-
action letters) that agent-of-payee arrangements are not subject to licensure. Examples include:
Illinois (prior to 2024): Before Illinois’ new law was passed, the Illinois Department of Financial &
Professional Regulation (IDFPR) issued a 2015 guidance titled “Statement Regarding Third-Party
Payment Processors and the TOMA”, and later a 2020 “Non-Binding Statement 2020-6”, confirming that a
payment processor acting as an agent of merchants (with an appropriate contract) is not required to
be licensed . The IDFPR essentially read the existing statute’s definition of “money transmission”
in light of agency law, concluding that such a processor is not receiving money for transmission to a
third party – it is receiving money on behalf of the merchant. This guidance filled the gap until the
law could be updated.
Connecticut: Connecticut’s money transmitter statute did not explicitly incorporate the agent-of-
payee exemption even after many peers did. However, the Connecticut Banking Department has
taken a no-action stance in certain cases. In 2015, CT issued a no-action letter for a company acting
as an agent of merchants (under contract) to process payments, indicating it would not enforce
licensing in that scenario . And in 2022, Connecticut passed a law (Public Act 22-89) that, while not
fully adopting the model act, gave the Banking Commissioner authority to grant “recognition
exemptions”. The Commissioner has indicated informally that bona fide agent-of-payee models
qualify for such recognition. (In practice, many payment companies still seek written confirmation
from CT to be safe.)
Maryland: Maryland’s Money Transmission Act historically did not mention agent-of-payee, but in
2022 the Maryland Commissioner of Financial Regulation adopted regulations (following the model
law) to acknowledge the exemption. Maryland chose to do this by regulation rather than by statute.
COMAR 09.03.13 (effective 2023) provides that persons meeting the agent-of-payee criteria are not
engaged in money transmission. Thus, Maryland regulators now formally recognize the exemption
. This regulatory change came alongside Maryland’s adoption of other portions of the Model Act
via rulemaking.
Pennsylvania: Pennsylvania’s prior law was silent on agent-of-payee. In January 2019, the
Pennsylvania Department of Banking and Securities issued a policy statement on digital currencies
and money transmission. In that statement (PA Bulletin, Vol. 49, No. 4, 2019), the Department noted
that only transmission of “money” (defined as fiat currency) was regulated, and it clarified that
“providing payment processing services to facilitate payments for goods or services (where the goods/
services provider is the ultimate payee)” does not constitute money transmission under Pennsylvania
law. Essentially, PA asserted that if you’re processing payments on behalf of a merchant – even if
through virtual currency – you are not engaging in the business of transmitting money for a fee, but
rather facilitating a purchase. This was effectively an agent-of-payee interpretation. In 2022,
Pennsylvania enacted Act 129 of 2022 (Money Transmission Business Licensing Law modernization),
which took effect in 2023. The new law explicitly includes an agent-of-payee exemption very similar
to other states. Thus PA moved from an interpretive stance to an express exemption.

Massachusetts: Massachusetts is an example of a state that has been silent statutorily but likely
recognized in practice. The Massachusetts Division of Banks has not published formal guidance on
agent-of-payee. However, industry practitioners report that Massachusetts has not been requiring
licenses from pure payment processors for merchants (likely viewing those as not in the business of
money transmission on their own). Massachusetts considered adopting the Model Act in 2023–24; if
enacted, it would explicitly add the exemption. Until then, it remains a bit unclear, but most treat
Massachusetts as tolerating the agent-of-payee model through an implied interpretation.
Rhode Island: Rhode Island only partially adopted the Model Act in 2023. Initially, RI did not have an
agent-of-payee clause and had even been cited as a state requiring licenses for marketplace
platforms. In mid-2023, Rhode Island passed a law (2023 R.I. Pub. Laws ch. 73) adding a limited
agent-of-payee provision for government bill payment and certain fintech arrangements, but it is
narrower than the standard exemption. As of late 2024, Rhode Island was still regarded as a state
without a broad agent-of-payee exemption . Businesses often approach RI for specific no-action
relief if their model is clearly merchant payment processing.
(c) States Silent or Ambiguous (Likely Recognized): A few states have no explicit law or known guidance,
but by analogy or enforcement posture, we suspect they would honor the agent-of-payee concept. Often
these are states where no issues have been raised for known payment facilitator businesses, suggesting
tacit acceptance.
Montana: Montana is unique because it does not require a money transmitter license at all for general
money transfer business. Montana has no Money Transmission Act (it exempts “payment
instruments” and currency exchange is lightly regulated). Thus, Montana does not need an agent-
of-payee exemption – any company (other than perhaps those dealing in currency or crypto
specifically) can operate without a license in Montana . The result is that Montana-based
transactions are effectively exempt by default. (Montana is the lone state with no MTL regime .) If
Montana did regulate transmitters in the future, it would presumably follow the majority approach
on agent-of-payee.
Alaska: As of 2025, Alaska had not codified an agent-of-payee exemption. Legislation to adopt the
Model Act (which includes agent-of-payee) was considered in 2023 but did not pass . However, the
Alaska Division of Banking has unofficially indicated that it does not view pure merchant payment
processors as MSBs requiring licensing, provided there is a proper agency arrangement. There is no
formal rule, so Alaska is somewhat ambiguous. (Troutman Pepper’s 2024 review lists Alaska among
states without the exemption on the books , so caution is warranted. It’s likely to be addressed in
a future law.)
Oklahoma: Oklahoma has been silent statutorily (no explicit exemption in the Oklahoma Financial
Transaction Reporting Act). It was on the list of states lacking the exemption as of mid-2024 . That
said, many PayFacs and fintechs operate in Oklahoma on the assumption that agent-of-payee is a
valid legal theory under common law. Oklahoma has not taken enforcement action to contradict that
assumption to our knowledge. It falls into the “likely recognized but not formally acknowledged”
bucket. (Oklahoma lawmakers have a draft bill to update the Money Services Act in 2025, which is
expected to include an agent-of-payee clause.)

New Hampshire and Maine: Both states updated their laws in recent years. Maine’s 2024 law
explicitly included agent-of-payee . New Hampshire adopted a version of the model act earlier
(2017), so it does have the exemption now. Prior to that, NH was silent but presumed to allow it
because the state’s definition of money transmitter excluded transactions “integral to a business sale
of goods or services.” Now it’s explicit.
Silent but likely safe: States such as Utah, Mississippi, Delaware have been holdouts in adopting
the exemption (Utah, Mississippi as of 2024 had no such statute; Delaware’s money transmitter law is
old and makes no mention of it). These states are sometimes categorized as not recognizing agent-
of-payee . However, their regulatory agencies have not actively challenged major payment
processors operating under an agency model. It may be that they tacitly rely on the “integral to sale
of goods” concept. Because there is no affirmative guidance, we classify them as unclear. In
practice, companies often include these states in their MTL licensing roster or use a partner model to
cover them, to be cautious.
(d) States Expressly Rejecting or Not Recognizing the Exemption: These are jurisdictions where either
the law or the regulator has affirmatively indicated that an agent-of-payee arrangement will not excuse the
licensing requirement. As of 2025, this was a short list. According to industry surveys, the remaining states
without an agent-of-payee rule (statute or policy) were: Alabama, Alaska, Delaware, District of Columbia,
Florida, Mississippi, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Oregon, Rhode Island, Utah, Wyoming . Among these,
a few stand out for having expressly taken a contrary position:
Florida: Florida’s Office of Financial Regulation (OFR) has explicitly refused to adopt the agent-of-
payee theory. In 2020, a payment processor (TandP, LLC) petitioned the OFR for a declaratory
statement that its agent-of-merchant model was not money transmission. The Florida OFR rejected
the petition and stated that Florida law “does not exempt an ‘agent of a payee.’” . The OFR’s
order held that if a business receives funds from a payer and then transmits those funds (even to a
merchant who appointed it as agent), that activity constitutes money transmission under Florida law
. Florida’s view is that the statute (Florida Money Services Act, Ch. 560) has a comprehensive list
of exemptions and agent-of-payee is not among them, so it cannot be read in by interpretation
. As a result, companies like payment facilitators must obtain a Florida MTL license or operate
under a licensed entity to serve Florida customers. Florida’s stance is considered one of the most
stringent and is a clear rejection of the exemption.
New York: New York is an interesting case. For years, NY did not recognize an agent-of-payee
exemption in its statutory law (NY Banking Law Article 13-B). However, New York does have a
regulatory provision that mirrors agent-of-payee for bill pay agents of utilities. Specifically, 3 NYCRR
§406.1 defines “agent of a payee” as “any person authorized by a payee to receive funds on behalf of the
payee and to deliver such funds to the payee.” and provides that a duly authorized agent of a payee
is exempt when receiving payments for that payee. This regulation has existed for some time, mainly
to allow agents of utilities and other billers to operate without a license. In recent enforcement
matters, however, the New York State Department of Financial Services (DFS) has been very skeptical
of entities self-designating as agent-of-payee. A notable case is MTACC, Inc. v. NYDFS (2015), involving
a company that facilitated payments from U.S. companies to foreign contractors. MTACC argued it
was an agent of the payee (the foreign client) and thus exempt. DFS determined MTACC did not
qualify because its user agreement imposed “numerous conditions and…significant discretion and
control” over the funds, inconsistent with a true agent-of-payee . For example, MTACC could

Arizona and New Mexico: Up until 2023, Arizona did not have an agent-of-payee clause; however,
Arizona enacted the Money Transmitter Modernization Act in 2023 (effective Jan 2024) which did
include the exemption. So Arizona moved into category (a). New Mexico still lacks any exemption
language and has not publicly embraced the concept. New Mexico’s regulators have quietly required
licenses for some merchant payment models, effectively rejecting the exemption by omission. It
remains on the “no exemption” list , though there is hope it will update its law soon.
Others (DC, AL, MS, DE, WY, OR, UT): These jurisdictions have neither adopted the exemption nor
issued guidance allowing it. They are effectively “no exemption” states by default. For instance,
District of Columbia has a money transmitter law with no agent-of-payee clause and expects
licensing of payment intermediaries. Alabama and Mississippi have been slower to modernize their
statutes; as of 2025 neither had an agent-of-payee provision and both are generally treated as
requiring licenses unless an express exemption (like for banks) applies. Delaware similarly has not
adopted it (Delaware’s MTL law is narrow but does cover receiving money for transmission).
Wyoming updated some digital asset laws but did not add agent-of-payee in its money transmitter
act – so it’s still required to license payment processors. Oregon had bills proposed but not passed;
thus no exemption there yet (Oregon’s DFCS tends to require licensing for marketplace payment
models in absence of a specific exemption).
In these “no exemption” states, if you act as an intermediary handling customer funds, you generally
must be licensed. Many of these states simply have not updated their laws since the agent-of-payee
concept gained traction. We anticipate some of them (e.g. Alabama, Mississippi) will adopt the Model Act in
coming years, bringing them in line with the majority.
(e) Jurisdictions Unclear or Case-by-Case: A few places defy easy categorization. They don’t have a clear
law or blanket policy, and treatment may depend on specifics or even personnel:
Connecticut (before the no-action stance) was one example of ambiguity; we covered it under (b)
since a no-action letter exists for agent-of-payee.
Puerto Rico and U.S. Territories: Puerto Rico’s money services law does not explicitly list agent-of-
payee, and the regulator there has not opined on it publicly – so it’s unclear (most fintechs obtain a
Puerto Rico MTL as a precaution). Guam and U.S. Virgin Islands similarly have old statutes without
mention; no known guidance.
Nevada (pre-2021): Before Nevada’s 2021 update, it was unclear. Now resolved with explicit
exemption.
Virginia: Virginia passed an agent-of-payee exemption in 2018, so it’s firmly category (a) now
(previously it was likely via interpretation).

In summary, the trend is overwhelmingly toward recognizing agent-of-payee. As of late 2024, only
about a dozen U.S. jurisdictions held out, and even some of those were considering legislation . The
Conference of State Bank Supervisors (CSBS) published an “Agent of the Payee Exemption Map” that shows 22
states allowing it by statute and 3 by case-by-case as of a couple years ago – those numbers have
only grown. The Model Money Transmission Modernization Act, released in 2021, has been a catalyst: it
expressly includes an agent-of-payee exemption, and virtually every state implementing the Model Act has
adopted that piece .

Common Qualifying Requirements for Agent-of-Payee Status
While each state’s law may have its own phrasing, there is a strong common core of requirements to qualify
as an agent-of-payee. These essential elements include:
Written Agency Agreement: Virtually every state requires a preexisting written contract between the
payee (merchant/service provider) and the agent. The contract should expressly appoint the
intermediary as the authorized agent of the payee for the purpose of receiving payments
. It’s important that the agreement be in place before any payments are taken (hence
“preexisting”). This contract will typically outline the duties of the agent, the scope of authority, and
the mechanism of funds remittance. For example, California’s law specifically references a
“preexisting written contract” with such terms , and Georgia’s statute itemizes that there must be a
written agreement directing the agent to collect payments on the payee’s behalf .
What must the contract contain? Key provisions include: a clear appointment of the company as an
agent to accept payments on behalf of the payee; an acknowledgment that the payee is bound by
the agent’s actions within that authority; a clause stating that payment to the agent constitutes
payment to the payee (more below); and often details on settlement timing, fees, and liabilities. (See
Sample Contract Provisions in a later section for a checklist.)
“Payment to Agent = Payment to Payee” Clause: This is crucial. The agreement (and often the
state law itself) must provide that when the agent receives money from the customer (payor), it is
deemed received by the payee. In other words, the customer’s obligation to the payee is extinguished
at that moment . This concept is drawn straight from agency law and is what distinguishes an
agent-of-payee transaction from a normal money transmission. If the payee is considered paid
immediately, the funds the agent holds are effectively the payee’s funds, not the customer’s funds
being sent to someone else. Nearly all states articulate this requirement. For instance, Illinois’
guidance required that the merchant’s customer be relieved of liability once payment is made to the
agent . Georgia’s statute explicitly says the payor’s obligation must be satisfied and “there is
no risk of loss to the payor if the agent fails to remit” . That latter phrase is important: the payor
should not have to pay twice; if the agent absconds, the loss falls on the payee (or the agent and
payee contractually).
Provider of Goods or Services (Qualifying “Payee”): The payee on whose behalf the agent is acting
generally must be a merchant, creditor, or other provider of non-money-transmission goods or
services. The exemption is typically framed as applying to payments “for goods or services (other than
money transmission services)” . This means that the model works for, say, a payment processor
collecting money for a retailer, landlord, utility company, lender, etc. It does not apply if the ultimate
payee is just receiving the money to forward it onward (that would be money transmission by the
payee). Many statutes define “payee” as “the provider of goods or services who is owed the
payment” . For example, California’s DFPI regulations clarify “payee means the provider of goods
or services, who is owed payment from the payor for the goods or services” . So the person or
entity receiving the funds via the agent must be selling something or collecting a debt (like a loan
payoff, utility bill, etc.). If the “payee” is just a person who’s receiving money for no goods/services
(e.g. a friend or a generic money transfer), the exemption does not cover that – those are person-to-
person remittances, not agent-of-payee scenarios. In short, the payee can be a business or
individual, but they must be owed the funds for providing a product, service, or credit. (Some

Agent Cannot Be Agent of the Payor: The flip side of the above – the intermediary must be acting
on behalf of the payee only, not on behalf of the payer. Many laws explicitly exclude an “agent of the
payor” from the exemption . For instance, California’s regulation states the exemption “does not
apply to an agent of a payor or sender.” If the intermediary were actually appointed by the
customer, or owes duties to the customer as their agent, then the payment to the intermediary
would not discharge the customer’s debt (and the logic collapses). Therefore, businesses should
carefully structure relationships so that they are clearly the payee’s agent. They may still provide a
good user interface for payors, but legally the arrangement and flow of funds should reflect payee-
agency. One practical test: if the intermediary were to fail, who bears the loss – if it’s the merchant
(payee) rather than the consumer, that indicates the intermediary was the merchant’s agent.
No Commingling / Segregation of Funds: While not always codified in the exemption statutes, it is
generally expected (and is good practice) that the agent-of-payee keep the funds collected separate
from its own operating funds. Some state regulatory guidance has hinted at this requirement. For
example, in Illinois’ 2020 non-binding opinion, the IDFPR noted that the processor would collect
payments on behalf of merchants and settle them to merchants, implying the funds were held in trust or
segregated accounts during the interim . Georgia’s rules require the agent-of-payee to hold funds
in a manner with “no risk of loss to the payor” – practically, this means safeguarding the funds
(often via a dedicated account or clearing account). While not every state statute mandates a trust
account, segregation is strongly advised. Many agents-of-payees use FBO accounts (“for the
benefit of” accounts at a bank) to hold customer payments on behalf of merchants until settlement.
This aligns with the notion that those funds are the payee’s funds, not to be used for the agent’s own
purposes. Additionally, keeping the funds segregated helps prove to regulators (and courts) that the
agent was not treating the money as its own or mixing it with unrelated revenues, which bolsters the
agency argument.
Timely Remittance / Settlement: Although the exemption statutes don’t usually set a specific time
frame (they focus on the legal relationship rather than timing), in practice an agent-of-payee is
expected to remit the funds to the payee promptly per the contract. Excessive delays could
undermine the argument that the funds are effectively the payee’s. Some state regulators informally
say that if an intermediary holds funds for an extended period or for purposes other than
transferring to the payee, that starts to look like money transmission. A common industry standard
is to settle to the payee daily or on a short cycle (e.g. 2-3 business days). A few states have implied
timing requirements in other contexts – for example, California’s DFPI in opinion letters has
sometimes asked how quickly the agent credits the merchant’s account. Immediate credit in
accounting (even if actual bank transfer happens next day) is ideal. The key is that the payee can
treat the customer as paid even if the funds are still in transit from agent to payee. If an agent were
to hold funds for, say, 60 days, that begins to strain the concept (and might trigger escheatment or
other issues too).

made aware that the intermediary is acting on behalf of the merchant and that their payment
obligation is satisfied. While not universal, this is a best practice. For instance, California DFPI
opinion letters often note that the merchant’s terms of sale or the payment interface should state that

Scope of Activities / Limitations: The agent-of-payee exemption generally covers the activity of
receiving and transferring payments to the payee for the intended goods/services. It does not
cover other activities like holding custody of funds for extended periods, offering stored balance
accounts, peer-to-peer transfers, currency exchange, etc. Many state laws explicitly limit the
exemption to the collection and processing of a payment for goods or services . If an agent starts
doing things outside that scope (e.g. taking funds from consumers to hold as a balance or to send to
other individuals), those transactions would not be exempt. Additionally, some states have clarified
special cases: e.g., California’s regulation excludes using the agent-of-payee exemption for “stored
value” issuers – meaning you can’t avoid a stored-value issuer license by claiming to be the agent
of all cardholders or something along those lines. The general rule: stick to facilitating payments
from a buyer to a seller. Activities beyond that may require licensing.
Financial Responsibility and Liability: While the agent-of-payee itself is exempt from licensing (and
thus usually not subject to bonding, net worth, examinations, etc., under the money transmitter
law), many agency contracts and some state interpretations effectively shift liability to the agent for
performance. For example, in the TandP (Florida) case, the petitioner noted that if it fails to pay the
merchant, the merchant’s only recourse is against the agent (TandP), not the consumer . This
is aligned with agency law – the agent who fails to turn over funds to the principal breaches its duty
and is liable to the principal, but the consumer should not be on the hook again. Some states might
expect evidence that the agent has the financial ability to fulfill its obligations to payees (though not
formally a licensing requirement). Thus, maintaining adequate funds and not becoming
insolvent is implicitly important: if an agent-of-payee fails financially and doesn’t pay merchants, the
consumers could be harmed indirectly (merchants might pursue consumers, or at least there’s
disruption). In extreme cases, a state could argue the arrangement was not in good faith or require
future licensing. Therefore, agents-of-payees often voluntarily adopt certain safeguards (insurance,
bonding, reserve funds) even if not legally mandated, to ensure they can always remit what’s owed
to merchants.
Recordkeeping and Audit Trails: Even when exempt from licensing, companies should keep
detailed records of transactions performed under the agent-of-payee model. Many states’ money
transmitter laws impose recordkeeping requirements on licensees, but an exempt entity won’t be
examined regularly. However, in the event of a question or investigation, being able to demonstrate
with records that each payment was for a good/service and was forwarded to the payee is critical.
Some states like Connecticut, when giving no-action relief, require periodic reporting or the ability
to inspect records to ensure ongoing compliance (this was mentioned in a Connecticut no-action
assurance in the past). Also, note that although the company may not be a licensed transmitter, it
could still be subject to federal AML requirements if it’s deemed a third-party processor (FinCEN
might not require MSB registration if fully exempt, but if any transactions fall outside the exemption,

consumer at risk of non-payment. Meet those conditions, and you have a very strong case for the
exemption in the vast majority of states .
Practical Applications and Scenarios
The agent-of-payee exemption is widely used in modern payment models. Here are several common
scenarios and how the exemption applies:
Payment Facilitators (PayFacs): This refers to processors that onboard merchants under a master
merchant account (common in card processing, e.g. Stripe, Square). PayFacs typically collect
customer payments (via credit card, ACH, etc.) on behalf of their sub-merchant clients and then settle
to those clients. Agent-of-payee is essentially the legal theory behind the PayFac model – the
PayFac acts as the agent of each sub-merchant (the payee) to accept payments. Card network rules
already require PayFacs to have written agreements with merchants and assume responsibility for
funding them. By structuring as agent-of-payee, PayFacs like Stripe have been able to operate in
many states without individual money transmitter licenses for each merchant payment flow. For
example, Stripe processes a payment from a buyer to an online seller: Stripe’s terms state it is
collecting on behalf of the seller, and the seller’s buyer is debt-free once payment is made to Stripe.
As long as this is clearly established, states with the exemption do not require a license. In states like
Florida (no exemption), Stripe and similar PayFacs have obtained money transmitter licenses or
partnered with a licensed entity to cover those transactions. The PayFac arrangement is one of the
most common use-cases of the agent-of-payee concept. It allows small merchants to accept
electronic payments without themselves having merchant bank accounts or licenses; the PayFac
centralizes the process. Card network operating regulations explicitly acknowledge this structure
(though they don’t use the term “agent-of-payee”, they allow “Payment Service Providers” to
aggregate payments for sponsored merchants).
Online Marketplaces and Gig Platforms: Marketplaces like Airbnb, Uber, Etsy, eBay, Amazon
Marketplace, etc., handle payments between buyers and sellers (or riders and drivers). They almost
always invoke agent-of-payee. For instance, Airbnb’s terms designate Airbnb Payments as the agent
of the host (payee) to collect funds from guests . When a guest pays Airbnb, the host’s
obligation to that guest is considered fulfilled – the host will get paid from Airbnb later. This allows
Airbnb to avoid being seen as transmitting guest funds to hosts (which would normally require
licenses in 50 states). Many states explicitly cite “marketplace facilitator platforms” in discussions of
agent-of-payee. The exemption fits naturally because the platform is providing a service (facilitating
a short-term rental or ride) and handling the money as part of that service. However, caution: In a
few states, marketplace platforms have faced scrutiny. New York DFS, for example, reportedly
required Uber to get a New York transmitter license around 2016 when Uber’s volume grew large,
because NY had no formal exemption. (Uber did so quietly via a subsidiary.) But in states like
California, Uber and Lyft rely on agent-of-payee (CA explicitly exempted them via the 2014 law
change). Real-world example: When you take an Uber ride in California, your payment is processed by

facilitate payments on behalf of independent sellers or service providers. Examples: A cloud kitchen
platform that collects orders and payments for member restaurants, or a salon management
software that allows stylists to charge clients through the app. These platforms effectively operate
like PayFacs or marketplaces. The agent-of-payee model can shield them from licensing as long as
they only handle payments that go to the service providers for services rendered. They will need
to sign agreements with each provider, stating the platform is authorized to accept customer
payments on the provider’s behalf. This is very common now – many vertical SaaS products have
integrated payments and rely on this exemption (often via a registered PayFac or with a sponsor
bank). One nuance: If the platform charges the end-customer directly for its own fees (not just on

own right for the fee, which is fine because you don’t need a license to collect your own fees). The
portion that is destined for the provider flows through under agent-of-payee. SaaS platforms should
also be mindful of fee splitting rules in certain industries (e.g. healthcare, legal) – but that’s outside
money transmission law. From a pure MTL perspective, as long as they adhere to agent-of-payee
principles, they avoid being a “money transmitter.” States like Illinois explicitly mentioned companies
offering “a digital platform to facilitate customer-to-merchant payments” as qualifying for the
exemption .
Invoice Factoring and Accounts Receivable Financing: These involve a third party purchasing or
handling invoices and collecting payments from the customer/debtor. In true factoring, the factor
purchases the receivable, so when it collects from the customer, it’s collecting a debt owed to itself –
that is not money transmission (the factor is the payee by assignment). However, some receivables
finance or management arrangements stop short of true sale. For example, a service might manage
collection of invoices on behalf of a company for a fee (without taking ownership of the receivables).
If such a service directly handles customer payments, agent-of-payee can be used: the service
becomes agent for the company (the payee) to collect invoice payments. Many states would consider
that exempt, since the service is just helping the company get paid for goods it sold or services it
rendered. That said, factors often just buy the debt and thus step into the shoes of the payee
outright – eliminating any transmission by legal construction. If structured as a pure factor, even
states without agent-of-payee exemptions might not require a license (because the payment isn’t to
a third party, it’s to the factor who owns the debt). But where the arrangement is commission-based
or the factor doesn’t assume full risk, using an agency framework is wise. One caution: in invoice
factoring scenarios, if the factor or platform holds funds on behalf of the seller (for example, it
collects payments then waits to remit minus fees on a weekly basis), as long as that relationship is
clearly one of agent holding principal’s funds, it should be exempt in AoP states. In non-AoP states,
one might argue it’s not transmission because the factor is an assignee – but that could be a gray
area if not a true assignment. It’s safer to either become licensed or structure as actual purchase of
receivables.

from many individuals to give to a project creator or cause. How do they avoid being money
transmitters? Often through agent-of-payee. For reward or donation crowdfunding: the platform
acts as the agent of the campaign creator (the payee) to collect funds from backers (payors). When

Property Management Companies (Rent Collection): Property managers often collect rent from

other laws (sometimes under real estate licensure exemptions). Even if not explicit, the agent-of-
payee logic applies: the property manager, by lease or management agreement, is the agent of the
landlord (payee) to receive rent. Thus, payment to the manager is legally payment to the landlord.
For instance, Florida (despite not exempting general agent-of-payee) actually has a separate carve-

are not treated as money transmitters. In states without a specific carve-out, the general agent-of-
payee exemption (if the state has one) should cover it: rent is payment for the housing service
provided by the landlord (payee), and the manager with a contract can collect as agent. One caveat:
If the property manager commingles many clients’ rents and holds them long-term, that could raise
issues (and might violate real estate trust account rules). Best practice is they deposit rents into a
trust account and disburse to owners promptly. We haven’t seen states prosecuting property
managers as unlicensed transmitters, likely because either explicit exemptions or the acceptance of
the agency principle.
Bill Payment Services (non-bank): Think of walk-in bill pay centers or apps that let you pay your
utility or phone bill. These services typically rely on being an agent of the biller (the utility, telecom,
etc.). Many billers actually appoint payment agents (e.g. Ace Cash Express acting as agent for the
electric company to accept payments). Under agent-of-payee statutes, such an agent is exempt
. FinCEN in 2008 addressed a case of a gift shop taking utility payments as authorized agent of
the utilities, and concluded it was not a money transmitter because it was acting on behalf of the
billers (payees) . States concur – this scenario was a classic impetus for agent-of-payee rules.
One nuance: some independent bill pay services don’t have formal agreements with each biller;

get licensed or use the federal “bill payment service” exemption if they meet it. But increasingly,
large billers partner with networks that designate agents. If you operate a bill pay app that pays
various billers on behalf of users, you’d want to secure agreements with those billers (or partner with
someone who has them) to safely use AoP. Otherwise, you may fall under licensure in some states.

your business model includes moving funds on behalf of payors (like businesses paying vendors or
payroll), check if an agent-of-payor exemption exists (about 4–5 states have those, separate from
agent-of-payee). Otherwise, that may still require licensing or use of a licensed conduit (since agent-
of-payee won’t apply when you’re on the payor side by definition).
In all these scenarios, the agent-of-payee structure reduces regulatory friction by eliminating the
“money transmission” from a legal standpoint. It effectively recasts what would be a three-party payment
into a two-party payment (customer -> payee, facilitated by agent). As long as the conditions are met,
businesses in these scenarios can scale nationally without obtaining 40+ state licenses – with the big
exception of those few states that won’t honor the concept. In practice, many companies still obtain a handful
of state licenses to cover the outliers (e.g. a common approach is to get licensed in Florida, New York,
Illinois (until now), perhaps Hawaii if needed, etc., and use AoP elsewhere). But the availability of the
exemption has been a major boon for fintech innovation – it’s explicitly cited in legal commentary as
removing “stifling” regulatory burden on new payment models .
Enforcement, Case Law, and Regulatory Pitfalls
While the agent-of-payee exemption is well-established, it is not a free pass – regulators will look past form
to substance. There have been instances where companies claimed the exemption but were told by
regulators that they do not qualify. Understanding these cases helps identify warning signs that an agent-of-
payee structure may not be respected:
New York DFS and MTACC, Inc.: As mentioned, MTACC was a payment platform for international
freelancers that attempted to use agent-of-payee to avoid New York licensing. DFS’s response (July
2013 letter) was that MTACC’s arrangement failed the exemption because of the platform’s level of
control over funds and account terms . Specifically, DFS noted MTACC’s user agreement
allowed it to return funds to the original sender, suspend accounts, etc., meaning the freelancers
(payees) did not have unconditional right to the funds upon MTACC’s receipt . In DFS’s view,
MTACC was interjecting itself into the payment flow beyond a mere passive agent. The court order
denying MTACC’s injunction further notes that NY’s exemption is for an agent “solely” of the payee
with a “passive role” – implying if the intermediary adds conditions or actively intermediates
beyond simple collection and payout, DFS will not consider it passive. The lesson: Do not retain
unilateral control over funds or impose conditions that could prevent the payee from
ultimately getting paid. An agent-of-payee should ideally have a nearly automatic obligation to
remit to the payee, with minimal discretion.

Florida OFR Enforcement: While Florida simply doesn’t allow AoP, the TandP petition outcome
shows how an authority might articulate non-compliance. Florida said that when an entity
“interjects itself” by receiving funds into an account it controls (like an omnibus account) and then
moving them, that is money transmission regardless of what the contract says . Florida’s
stance hinged on the idea that the agent’s involvement was the very act of transmission (moving
funds from payer’s bank to payee’s bank). They didn’t give weight to agency in that analysis.
Companies operating in Florida have received cease-and-desist letters if they operate unlicensed,
even if they had AoP contracts elsewhere. (For example, a few early online rent payment startups got
notices from Florida about unlicensed activity prior to partnering with banks or getting licensed.) The
takeaway for enforcement in no-exemption states is straightforward: if you operate without a license,
you risk enforcement, period. No contract verbiage will save you there.
California DBO (pre-2014): Before California enacted its exemption, the regulator (then DBO)
actually rejected agent-of-payee arguments. A notable example: An early mobile payments company
argued that because it had agency agreements with online merchants (saying customer payment to
the company equaled payment to merchant), it wasn’t transmitting money. In 2013, California DBO
disagreed, stating “the fact that the customer’s liability to the merchant is satisfied when the bill is paid
has no bearing on whether money is being received for transmission” . They viewed the company
as “transmitting money between the customer and the merchant” . This strict view was overturned by
the 2014 statutory change, but it illustrates that regulators can be skeptical if the law doesn’t
explicitly support the exemption. In that case, CA basically said “we don’t care about your private
contract, we see funds moving from A to C via B – that’s transmission.” After the law was changed,
the same scenario became permissible. Lesson: Always check the current law – before a statute
exists, mere contract might not convince an agency. Since most big states now have the law, this is
less of an issue, but smaller states with old laws might still react like pre-2014 California.
State Examinations and Audits: Licensed money transmitters go through exams; an exempt agent-
of-payee, however, might still face scrutiny via state financial authority inquiries or complaints. If a
consumer complains, “I paid this platform and the merchant says they never got the money,” a
regulator may investigate. If it turns out the platform mismanaged funds or did not promptly remit,
the regulator could decide the platform was effectively engaged in unlicensed activity (especially in a
state where exemption criteria weren’t truly met). There haven’t been publicized enforcement
actions exactly like this, but it’s a plausible scenario. The best defense is to operate impeccably:
always remit funds, keep customers and merchants informed, and honor the agency obligations. If
you fail to pay the payee, you not only breach contract but also undermine the very rationale of the
exemption (since the payor then isn’t really cleared of liability, if the merchant doesn’t get paid and
comes after them – that’s a nightmare scenario that could prompt regulatory action or even fraud
claims).
Consumer Protection and UDAAP: Even if not prosecuted under money transmitter laws, a state AG
or CFPB could take action if an agent-of-payee structure is abused in a way that harms consumers.
For example, if an intermediary goes bankrupt holding many customer payments that never reach
merchants, that could draw unfair/deceptive practice claims. This hasn’t played out much because
most reputable payment companies either get licensed or are careful when using the exemption.
But it’s a risk to note – the exemption is not a shield against all liability; it just means you don’t need
that particular license. Other laws still apply.

Court Cases Interpreting Agent-of-Payee: There is relatively little case law directly on point, as

Consent Orders Involving Failed AoP Claims: To date, we haven’t seen a public consent order
solely about misusing the agent-of-payee exemption. However, a hypothetical example could be: A
company thought it was exempt, didn’t get licenses, but a regulator found it didn’t meet criteria and
treated it as unlicensed activity. The resolution could be a consent order where the company agrees

ended up obtaining licenses rather than pushing an agent-of-payee theory (even though their
activities might have fit it in some states). This suggests big players often choose the conservative
route to avoid enforcement entanglements.
Warning Signs Your Structure May Not Be Respected: In light of enforcement and guidance, here are red
flags:
No true agency contract or poor documentation: If you cannot produce a clear, executed agency
agreement with each payee you serve, you’re at risk. Regulators will ask: “show us the contract that
makes you an agent.” If it’s absent or vague, the exemption may be denied.
Agent retains too much control or imposes conditions: If your terms allow you to hold funds indefinitely,
cancel transactions arbitrarily, or redirect funds back to payors without payee consent, you might be
deemed more than an agent. An agent-of-payee should operate on the payee’s behalf, not act as
another decision-maker in the chain. MTACC’s case is instructive – avoid “numerous conditions” that
give you outs in forwarding money .
Funds not promptly remitted or comingled: If you hold onto money for a long time, or mix customer
funds with your own, a regulator might say you’re effectively using those funds like a money
transmitter would. Immediate or at least scheduled remittances from a dedicated account are safer.
Payee not truly a provider of goods/services: If the so-called payee is actually just a pass-through or a
shell. For example, say a company tries to route international remittances by setting up a U.S.
“payee” entity that pretends to provide some service but really just forwards money abroad.
Regulators see through that. The exemption is not meant to cover classic remittances by artificially
inserting an intermediary as “payee.” In fact, FinCEN and states have shut down schemes where a
foreign exchange service claimed its local affiliate was the “payee” selling currency to customers –
those didn’t fly as agent-of-payee since the end purpose was still money transfer .
Handling of customer funds beyond payment: If the intermediary offers wallets or stored value where
customers keep a balance that can be used for various payments, that’s outside a simple agent-of-
payee transaction (that is ongoing money transmission or payment instrument issuance). Many
fintech apps do both: they have a wallet and also pay merchants. In such cases, only the direct

been slapped down (like money transfer companies trying to call themselves agents of payees in creative
ways). Staying within the spirit of the exemption – enabling transactions between buyers and sellers – is the
best way to avoid enforcement issues.
Structuring Best Practices for Agent-of-Payee Arrangements
To successfully implement an agent-of-payee model and maintain the exemption, companies should adhere
to several best practices in contracts, operations, and compliance. Here is a checklist of measures and
provisions:
1. Robust Agency Contract Provisions: Every contract with a payee (merchant or service provider) should
include certain essential clauses:
Appointment of Agent: A clear statement that the payee (merchant) appoints YourCompany as its
agent for the limited purpose of receiving payments from customers on the payee’s behalf .
Use explicit language of agency.
Authority to Receive Funds: Language that the payee authorizes and empowers YourCompany to
accept payments from payors, and that such acceptance will be treated as if the payee had received
the funds directly .
Obligation Discharge Clause: A clause stating that a customer’s payment to YourCompany satisfies
and extinguishes the customer’s payment obligation to the payee to the extent of the amount
received . This mirrors the statutory requirement and should also appear in any terms
presented to customers.
Liability and Risk of Loss: Specify that once YourCompany receives a payment, the payee bears the
risk of YourCompany’s non-remittance (i.e. payee won’t go after the customer if funds are lost) .
Often the contract will say the payee’s sole recourse for not getting funds is against YourCompany,
not against the customer . This aligns incentives and demonstrates no risk to the payer.
Funds Handling & Segregation: The contract can require YourCompany to hold payments in a
fiduciary capacity for the payee until remitted. It may mandate use of separate client accounts, etc.
While not legally required everywhere, including this can reassure regulators and payees.

agreements to tweak language to satisfy certain states (e.g. adding the specific phrase “payment to agent =
payment to merchant” if not already in the main contract).
2. Explicit Customer-Facing Disclosures: As noted, it’s wise to inform customers that the platform is acting
on behalf of the merchant. This can be in the Terms of Use or at checkout. For example: “XYZ is the
authorized payment agent of the merchant. Your payment to XYZ will be deemed payment to the merchant,
satisfying your obligation.” Such a notice, while not legally mandated in most places, accomplishes several
things: it provides transparency, could help mitigate customer confusion in event of disputes, and bolsters
the legal argument that the customer knew and agreed to the agency arrangement (which could stop a
customer from later claiming they still owed the merchant directly or that they were defrauded if merchant
wasn’t paid). California DFPI explicitly liked seeing this in a case they approved .
3. Operational Segregation of Funds: Implement systems to route incoming payments into dedicated
accounts (often called “trust accounts”, “FBO – for benefit of [MerchantName] accounts”, or clearing
accounts). Avoid mixing these with your company’s operating cash. Many payment providers open a pooled
FBO account at a bank that is titled something like “YourCompany as agent for its payees” and then use sub-
ledgering to track each merchant’s balance. This aligns with the fiduciary role. It also provides clarity in the
event of insolvency – those funds aren’t your property (in theory, they’re the payees’ funds held by an
agent). Proper segregation and bookkeeping can prevent a scenario where, say, a bankruptcy court would
treat customer payments as part of YourCompany’s estate. Segregation was implicitly touched on in the
Florida declaratory action: the OFR contrasted a pure data processing model (no funds touch the
processor’s account) vs. a model where funds do go through the processor’s account . The former
was fine; the latter triggered licensure. So to the extent possible, structure the flow so that funds move
directly from payer to an account for payees (even if you control that account as agent).
4. Prompt Settlement and Reconciliation: Have clear internal policies to settle funds to payees quickly
(e.g. T+1 or T+2 days). Automated reconciliation is important – you should always know whose funds you’re
holding at any time, and the amounts due. Avoid unnecessarily delaying payouts; only hold funds if needed
for transaction clearing or risk management (e.g. brief hold to ensure a payment isn’t fraudulent or doesn’t
bounce). If you do hold a reserve or rolling settlement for risk reasons, that should be spelled out in the
merchant agreement (so it’s still within the agency authority). Also, keep those withheld funds documented.
Prompt settlement not only keeps merchants happy but also reduces regulatory risk – it minimizes the float
that the agent has at any moment.

action from other fronts (FTC, etc.) which indirectly could complicate your status.

you’re dealing with a state known to be strict or ambiguous, consider seeking an official opinion or no-
action letter. Many states’ banking regulators will, upon request, issue a letter confirming whether a license
is needed for a described model. For example, you might write to Florida OFR (knowing they are strict) –
though in FL’s case they already indicated no exemption, but in a state like Connecticut or Alaska, a well-
crafted letter might yield a confirmation that you’re not required to license under the agent-of-payee logic.
Document those responses. If a state declines to give a clear answer, that itself is a sign you might need to
err on the side of licensing there. Additionally, if there’s a plan to expand into a new type of service not
squarely within agent-of-payee, get advice or an opinion before launching it. For instance, if your platform
that collects on behalf of merchants also wants to start holding consumer funds for future purchases (like a
stored balance or rewards program), that could cross into licensable territory – better to carve that out or
get licensed for that portion.

8. Monitor Legislative and Regulatory Changes: As this area is evolving, keep track of new laws. For
instance, if you relied on Illinois’ guidance in 2020, note that by 2025 there’s a new law with possibly

9. Prepare a Contingency Plan: What if something goes wrong? For example, if a major payee partner
goes bankrupt while you’re holding their customer payments, or if your own company has financial trouble.
Plan for how to manage those funds so that consumers aren’t harmed. In agency law, if an agent becomes
unable to perform, ideally the principal (payee) should step in to collect its funds. So think about including
in contracts what happens if YourCompany cannot continue – perhaps funds in transit revert to the payee
or an escrow arrangement. Having such a clause could even be part of a business continuity plan that you

10. Consider Partial or Full Licensing if Necessary: Despite best efforts, you might decide to obtain a
money transmitter license in certain key states as a precaution or for flexibility. For example, some
companies that mostly rely on AoP still get a license in New York (since NY’s exemption is narrow) and
Florida (no exemption). This dual approach means for transactions in those states, you are covered by your
license, whereas elsewhere you use the exemption. There’s nothing wrong with a hybrid approach – it can
even be necessary if part of your business doesn’t qualify for AoP. Just maintain proper segregation: if you
also do licensed transmission (like P2P payments), ensure you comply with those obligations (e.g. bonding)
for that side, while keeping your agent-of-payee operations distinct.
In sum, operational discipline and clarity in agreements are the cornerstones of a compliant agent-of-
payee setup. Most regulators have been reasonable with companies who can clearly articulate and
document their compliance with the exemption criteria. The issues tend to arise when a company operates
in a gray area or without sufficient transparency. Following these best practices significantly lowers the risk
of a regulator concluding that your “agent-of-payee” is just a thin veil over an unlicensed money transmitter.
Hybrid and Edge Cases: Managing Mixed Business Models
Often, businesses find that some transactions or product lines qualify as agent-of-payee, while others do
not. In such cases, a hybrid approach is needed – part reliance on exemption, part traditional licensing or
partnership. Here’s how to navigate situations where only a subset of your activities fall under agent-of-
payee:
Segregate Different Transaction Flows: If certain transactions are pure merchant payments (AoP-
eligible) and others involve person-to-person or other transfers (not AoP), separate them in your
operations and perhaps even in corporate structure. For example, you might have one entity solely
handling marketplace payments (and structured as agent-of-payee), and another entity or division
handling wallet or P2P services (which is a licensed money transmitter or an agent of a bank). By
segregating, you ensure that the AoP exemption isn’t “contaminated” by non-qualifying activities.
Also, if you’re ever questioned, you can clearly say “those other activities are done by our licensed
affiliate, not by the AoP entity.”

Disclose to Regulators in License Applications: If you do need to get licensed for part of your

business are exempt under agent-of-payee. Most state regulators will not object; they may even
annotate your file that certain services are outside their purview. However, they will expect you to
have controls to prevent cross-over. For instance, if you have a customer account product (licensed)
and a direct merchant pay product (exempt), ensure that customers can’t inadvertently use the
merchant pay flow to send money to a friend, etc., because that would be using an exempt channel
for a non-exempt purpose.
Example – Payment App with Multiple Functions: Suppose you run an app that a) lets users pay

selects a friend to send money, the flow actually goes through a different pathway (maybe your bank
partner debits and credits accounts) – not through the AoP accounts meant for merchant funds.
Internally, treat them distinctly. Possibly label the P2P service under a different brand or division to
emphasize the separation (some companies do this).
Geofencing by Jurisdiction: As a practical matter, some companies adjust their model in certain
states. For instance, a fintech might operate as agent-of-payee in 45 states, but in Florida, it route
payments differently (maybe directly through a clearing bank as Florida-licensed, or simply block
usage in FL except via a licensed partner). This is a form of state-by-state hybridization. It can be
technically complex to geofence features, but it might be simpler than obtaining licenses in some
cases. Many payment startups initially block signups from NY or FL until they get licensing sorted,
while serving other states under AoP. If you do this, you must ensure your app or service can reliably
detect user jurisdiction (by address, IP, or BIN of card, etc.) and enforce restrictions. It’s not ideal for
user experience, but it’s an interim solution companies have used.
Converting Exempt Transactions to Licensed Flow (and Vice Versa): In some workflows, a
transaction might start as an agent-of-payee scenario but then partially involve a further
transmission. For example, a digital wallet might allow loading funds via a credit card which goes to
pay off a user’s purchase to a merchant (AoP), but any leftover goes into stored value (which is then
outside AoP). You’ll need to split that transaction – treat the merchant portion under AoP and the
leftover under license. This requires careful funds accounting. A rule of thumb: whenever funds
won’t be promptly disbursed to a payee for goods/services, you’ve left the AoP domain. The
remaining funds become akin to customer stored funds, which is money transmission. So you might
auto-refund or prevent leftover balances in an AoP flow to avoid creating a transmission scenario. If
leftover balances or multi-hop flows are part of your business, you likely need a license or a different
exemption (like authorized delegate of a licensed e-money issuer, etc.).
Use of Authorized Delegates or Banks: One strategy if you don’t want multiple entities is to
partner with a licensed money transmitter or a bank for the non-AoP parts. For instance, some
payment processors become an authorized delegate of a licensed money transmitter (like
Western Union Business Solutions or similar) specifically in states where they can’t rely on AoP. In
those states, legally the transaction is conducted under the licensee’s authority. This patchwork can
be cumbersome but is sometimes used as a bridge. Another approach: partner with a bank to hold
and transfer any wallet/P2P funds (banks are exempt from state MTL, and if the bank is doing the

transfer, state law doesn’t apply). Many fintech apps use a national bank to custody wallet funds and
execute transfers between wallets, thus avoiding state licenses for that part, while the fintech itself
only directly handles the merchant payment side under AoP.
In all, a hybrid structure demands careful delineation of which rules apply to which transactions. Do not
assume that because part of your business is exempt, the entire business is off the hook. Regulators
will evaluate each activity stream. It’s entirely acceptable to have a dual approach – use the agent-of-payee
exemption where it fits, and obtain licenses or partners for where it doesn’t. That approach demonstrates a
prudent understanding of the law and can actually strengthen credibility: you’re not over-reaching the
exemption beyond what it was meant for.
Relationship to Other Exemptions and Frameworks
It’s useful to distinguish agent-of-payee from other overlapping concepts in payments law and how they
interact:
Bank Exemption vs. Agent-of-Payee: One common alternative to agent-of-payee is leveraging the
bank exemption. Banks (and in many states, trust companies or credit unions) are exempt from
money transmitter licensing . If a fintech can structure itself as a service provider to a bank
(rather than directly as an agent of the merchant), it might not need a license because the bank is
the entity transmitting money. For example, some payment companies route all funds through an
FBO account at a partner bank. The bank is essentially moving money from one sub-account to
another, and the fintech is just instructing it. In states, a bank is not a “person” that needs an MTL
license, so those movements are not regulated by the state MTL agencies. This is a different
exemption (the “bank agent” or bank service provider model). Some states explicitly exempt
agents of banks in their statutes (see Georgia’s exemption (17) for agents of entities exempt under
the bank exemption) . The bank exemption is powerful but requires a willing bank partner and
typically the bank must have a substantial role (not just renting its charter, to avoid regulatory
pushback).
Interaction: You can combine these – e.g., you act as agent-of-payee for merchants, and you use a bank
account to process the payments. Actually, that’s quite typical: an agent-of-payee will hold the funds in a
bank account (which is of course fine). The key is the role: if the bank is simply holding funds as a deposit,
you still rely on AoP for the licensing exemption; if the bank is actually the one debiting and crediting
accounts (like in a true “prepaid card” or “payment processor bank sponsor” model), then arguably the bank
is doing the transmitting. In practice, most fintechs prefer AoP or licensing, because the bank model often
triggers other issues (like the need for the bank to comply with brokered deposit or prepaid rules, etc.).
If you can fully align with a bank’s operations, that bank’s federal oversight might cover the activity. For
instance, early PayPal in some countries operated as agent of a bank. In the U.S., PayPal ended up getting
state licenses anyway, partly because not all states recognize a broad agent-of-bank exemption (though
many do as “authorized delegate of a bank” as in Georgia’s law ).
Comparison: Agent-of-payee and bank-agent are distinct but not mutually exclusive. The bank exemption is
generally more sweeping (banks can do any transmission business with no state license). Agent-of-payee is
narrower but available to non-banks. Sometimes when one fails, the other could step in. For example, if a
regulator argued your AoP structure doesn’t hold up in their state, you might pivot to a model where a

dependency and oversight by bank regulators (and banks will require their fintech partners to adhere to
lots of compliance rules).
Authorized Delegate / Agent of a Licensed Transmitter: Many licensed money transmitters
appoint authorized delegates (also called agents) to act on their behalf in selling or issuing
instruments or sending money. This is a different concept than agent-of-payee. For instance, Western
Union appoints convenience stores as authorized delegates to receive money from customers. The
delegate doesn’t need its own license because it operates under Western Union’s license and
Western Union is liable for the delegate’s actions . This is effectively a franchise model for
licensed MSBs.
Comparison: Agent-of-payee exemption means you don’t need any license at all because of the way the
transaction is structured. Being an authorized delegate means you are piggybacking on someone else’s
license. The authorized delegate model might cover scenarios AoP doesn’t – for example, pure P2P
transfers could be done via you becoming an agent of an existing licensed transmitter like MoneyGram. But
then you have contractual obligations, reporting to that licensee, and you are not entirely free – the
principal licensee must ensure your compliance .
For companies that qualify for AoP, using the delegate model is usually unnecessary (why share revenue or
control if you can be exempt on your own?). However, if you operate in states with no AoP exemption, one
workaround is to sign on as an agent of a big licensed transmitter in just those states. That way, you still
avoid getting your own license there. This patchwork is complex but possible. Some FinTechs have done
deals with license holders to cover, say, NY and FL business under the partner’s licenses (with the FinTech
acting as a delegate in those states), while using AoP in others. The delegate approach requires the licensed
entity to be comfortable and regulators to approve the relationship, so it’s not always faster or cheaper than
just getting a license oneself.
Note: When the Model Money Transmitter Act was created, it kept both concepts separate – it has an
agent-of-payee exemption and still assumes the traditional authorized delegate system remains for licensed
transmitters . They serve different needs: agent-of-payee for direct merchant payments, authorized
delegates for classic remittance networks.
Payment Processor Exemption vs Agent-of-Payee: Some state and federal laws refer to a “payment
processor” or “integral” exemption that is conceptually similar but framed differently. FinCEN’s four-
prong test we discussed is sometimes called the “payment processor exemption” . Some states (like
Georgia in code section (13) after the AoP clause ) have an exemption for a person acting as an
intermediary processing payments between a creditor who has an outstanding obligation and the
debtor, provided the creditor is licensed or exempt and certain conditions are met. This is actually an
exemption aimed at things like third-party payment collectors in finance (e.g., someone collecting
loan payments on behalf of a lender) – which is essentially an agent-of-payee scenario, but they
describe it differently. Georgia’s clause (13) , for instance, says if you’re processing payments on
behalf of an entity that incurred a money transmission obligation to a sender, and you have a
contract with that entity and you’re responsible to make the sender whole if something goes wrong,
you’re exempt. That’s a mouthful, but it’s targeting payment processors operating in the background for
other licensed entities. It overlaps with agent-of-payee but is not identical – it’s more like an agent for
an MSB exemption or a Mercury exemption (named after a company and an old FinCEN ruling).

In simpler terms: Payment processor exemption often refers to scenarios where the money transmission
is ancillary to another service (like bill pay, payroll, credit card acquiring). Agent-of-payee is one flavor of
that – arguably the most clean-cut flavor, focusing on merchant payments. But states or FinCEN might have
separate exemptions for things like operators of payments systems (clearinghouses), payroll processors
(agent-of-payor), etc. If you qualify as agent-of-payee, you likely qualify as a payment processor under
FinCEN’s rule. But the reverse isn’t always true; some processors might not meet the exact agent-of-payee
test but still be exempt e.g. because they only transmit between banks or something.
For practical purposes, the agent-of-payee exemption is the realization of the “payment processor
exemption” in most use-cases, as FinCEN itself indicated by referencing merchant payment processors in its
2008 and 2012 rulings . Companies shouldn’t assume they have two separate exemptions to choose
from – it’s more that the law uses different terminology in places. To avoid confusion, best to frame yourself
as agent-of-payee if that fits; if not, see if you fit another specific exemption like “payment system operator”
or “agent of bank” etc.
Third-Party Sender (ACH) vs Agent-of-Payee: In the ACH network, an intermediary who initiates
debits on behalf of a company (the Originator) is called a Third-Party Sender (TPS) under NACHA
rules. Many agent-of-payee processors act as TPS – e.g., if you pull money from a customer’s account
for a merchant via ACH, you as the platform might be the TPS between the merchant (Originator)
and the ODFI bank. This TPS status has no direct bearing on state licensing; it’s an ACH operational
designation. However, NACHA rules require TPS’s to register and conduct due diligence, etc. The TPS
concept and agent-of-payee often coincide: a PayFac might be a TPS for its merchants in ACH
transactions.
One might ask: if I’m a TPS in ACH, do I need a money transmitter license? The answer goes back to the
same analysis – if those ACH payments are being made to payees for goods/services and you meet the
agent-of-payee criteria, then under state law you likely don’t need a license (in AoP states). But NACHA
doesn’t care about that; NACHA cares that the ODFI knows you’re a TPS and monitors you. So you have dual
compliance: follow NACHA rules for TPS (e.g., annual audit requirements if volume is high, registration with
NACHA) and follow state law for AoP. There’s a synergy though: NACHA’s concept assumes the TPS has a
formal agreement with the Originator (merchant) – which you do if you’re agent-of-payee. And TPS’s often
have direct debit authority from the consumer (or are passing through the merchant’s debit authorization).
FinCEN’s conditions also talk about submitting debits authorized by the customer to the bank etc. –
basically describing an ACH third-party processing scenario.
In summary, being a Third-Party Sender under NACHA is not an exemption from state licensing, but if you’re
a TPS for merchant payments, you likely align with agent-of-payee usage. Many regulators, when seeing a
company is a NACHA-registered TPS for say utilities or merchants, will understand that it’s a payment
processor model (some might still require a license in states like FL though). It’s good to mention your TPS
status to regulators because it shows you are integrated into the banking system (one of FinCEN’s prongs is
using clearing systems with BSA-regulated institutions , which ACH is).
Also, being a TPS means you have to abide by NACHA’s rules on not commingling funds between different
Originators unless authorized – which dovetails with the segregation principle we discussed.
Interactions and Overlaps: Often a business might simultaneously be leveraging multiple exemptions. For
instance, a fintech might say: “We don’t need a license because (a) we qualify as agent-of-payee for these

One interesting aspect is that the CSBS Model Law tried to consolidate exemptions, including agent-of-

In Conclusion: Agent-of-payee is one tool in a larger toolbox of regulatory strategies. It interacts mostly
complementarily with others: you might use AoP for the core payment flows, and rely on bank/delegate
exemptions for the peripheral flows. Or if AoP doesn’t apply, you fall back on licensure or another
exemption. The key is not to mix them up or double count. For example, don’t try to claim both that you are
an agent-of-payee and that the payee is a licensed money transmitter so you’re their delegate – that would
confuse matters (and usually isn’t necessary). Use the appropriate concept for the scenario at hand.
Finally, note that nothing in agent-of-payee exemption negates federal obligations like FinCEN registration if
you were deemed an MSB for any part of your operations. Fortunately, FinCEN aligns pretty well – if you
truly meet their payment processor exemption, you’re not an MSB. But if any part of your business doesn’t,
you might have to register. Many companies that mostly do AoP still register with FinCEN as MSBs just to be
safe, especially if there’s ambiguity in some flows. Each exemption (state or federal) covers certain aspects,
so compliance folks look at them in tandem to ensure all bases are covered.

Conclusion
The agent-of-payee exemption has become a critical doctrine in money transmission law, enabling modern
payment intermediaries to operate efficiently while still protecting consumers. At the federal level,
FinCEN’s rules and rulings support these arrangements, carving out bona fide payment processors from
money transmitter status . State-by-state, we now have a majority consensus via statutes or
guidance that if you meet the key criteria (agency contract and assumption that payee is paid when agent is
paid), you do not need a transmitter license . A shrinking minority of states (like Florida and a few
others) remain opposed or silent, requiring careful planning or licensing for those jurisdictions .

When structuring an agent-of-payee model, prioritize clear legal agreements, sound operational
controls, and alignment with the exemption’s purpose – facilitating payments for real transactions, not
operating a separate money transfer business. Keep an eye on evolving laws (for instance, bills in states like
Alabama or Mississippi could soon tip them into the exemption column, simplifying compliance further).
And always remember: the exemption frees you from licensing, but not from the responsibility of handling
other people’s money prudently. States expect that even unlicensed agents-of-payees will conduct business
in a safe and honest manner, since ultimately it’s consumers and merchants who suffer if something goes
wrong.
By following the guidance and best practices outlined above, a company can confidently leverage the agent-
of-payee exemption to scale its payment services, all while staying on the right side of regulators. This
balanced approach fosters innovation in payments (as many have noted, rigid licensing of every
intermediary can stifle new models ) while still ensuring that when you pay, say, your Uber or your
Airbnb through a platform, you’re legally paying the driver or host – and if the platform never passes it
on, you as the consumer are not on the hook again . That is the essence of agent-of-payee: aligning
legal responsibility with the flow of commerce so that money transmitter laws focus on true remittances
and custodial money services, not the everyday facilitation of payments in our digital economy .
