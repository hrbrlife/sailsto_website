State-by-State Crypto MSB Licensing Exemptions
(December 2025)
Executive Summary
Cryptocurrency regulation in the United States remains a patchwork of state laws and interpretations,
especially regarding money transmission licensing. Our Montana-domiciled MSB (Money Services
Business) seeks a low-risk strategy, operating only where crypto activities are clearly exempt or permitted
with minimal compliance burden. This memo provides a 50-state analysis of crypto-related money
transmitter licensing exemptions, interpretations, and trends, with an emphasis on explicit and implicit
exemptions that reduce regulatory risk. We categorize each jurisdiction (50 states + DC) into Green, Yellow,
Orange, or Red tiers, reflecting how crypto-friendly their regulatory stance is. We then dive deeper into
favorable states and analyze practical scenarios (outbound payments, inbound receipts, white-label
operations, crypto-funded prepaid cards) under each category. Finally, we discuss regulatory trends (both
toward crypto-friendly and stricter regimes) and present strategic recommendations for our operations and
client services.
Key Findings: Many states do not treat pure cryptocurrency transactions as “money transmission”,
either by statute or regulatory guidance, meaning no state license is required for crypto-only activities
(Green tier). Other states have conditional exemptions or clear licensing frameworks for crypto (Yellow
tier). A number of states remain ambiguous or silent on crypto (Orange tier), creating uncertainty but with
little enforcement history. Several states explicitly include virtual currency in licensing laws and actively
enforce compliance (Red tier), requiring a money transmitter license or separate crypto license for most
crypto business activity.
Strategic Highlights:
Green States (Most Crypto-Friendly): No license required for crypto-only transactions. These include
states with explicit statutory exemptions (e.g. Wyoming, New Hampshire), no MSB laws at all
(Montana), or clear agency guidance that crypto is not “money” (e.g. Texas, Pennsylvania). We can
safely send/receive crypto and even facilitate client off-ramps in these jurisdictions without
triggering state licensing .
Yellow States (Conditional or Licensed-Friendly): Exemptions with conditions or straightforward
licensing paths. Some states require a specific structure or conditions for exemption – for example,
North Carolina exempts crypto transmission when acting as an agent-of-the-payee for a merchant
, and Florida now exempts two-party direct crypto payments from licensing . Other Yellow
states have dedicated crypto licensing regimes that, while requiring compliance, are clearly
defined (e.g. Louisiana’s Virtual Currency Business Act, California’s new Digital Assets law effective
July 2025 ). Operations are possible here with an appropriate structure or license.

Orange States (Unclear but Low Enforcement): No explicit law or guidance on crypto. These states
have ambiguous statutes (often defining “money” or “monetary value” broadly, which could include
crypto) but have not issued clear guidance or pursued enforcement against crypto-only services.
We treat these on a case-by-case basis, erring on the side of caution. Engaging in crypto
transactions in Orange states might not technically violate law if crypto isn’t clearly defined as
money, but formal legal opinions are advised to confirm risk.
Red States (Strict Regulation or Active Enforcement): Virtual currency clearly regulated as money
transmission. These include states that explicitly include crypto in the definition of money
transmission and have enforced compliance (e.g. Georgia, New York, New Jersey) . In Red
states, any crypto transmitting or exchange business requires a license (either a standard money
transmitter license covering virtual currency, or a separate crypto license like NY’s BitLicense ). We
will avoid operations in these states unless properly licensed, as unlicensed activity risks cease-
and-desist orders and legal penalties .
Recommendations: Focus operations and client interactions in Green states for maximum flexibility (e.g.
sending stablecoins to clients in Texas or Wyoming with no state license needed). Utilize agent or delegate
structures in Yellow states to meet exemption conditions (e.g. using our MSB license and agent-of-payee
models in North Carolina or an in-state banking partner in Florida). For Orange states, limit exposure or
seek no-action assurances from regulators where possible. Red states should be approached only with full
licensing or through licensed partners, or simply avoided due to high compliance costs. Consider forming
new series or subsidiaries in crypto-forward states (Wyoming, Texas, etc.) to leverage their friendly laws
for specific services (e.g. a Wyoming SPDI charter for custody or a Texas entity for stablecoin payments).

California RED (as
of 2025)
New Crypto License Regime – Historically, California did not
require crypto exchange MSBs to be licensed, as the regulator
had “not concluded whether cryptocurrencies are money”

services. However, this changed with the 2023 Digital
Financial Assets Law (DFAL), effective July 1, 2025 . DFAL

expected once the law is in effect.

trigger licensing. Use a limited model (direct sales) to
operate without a license, or obtain a license for full exchange

the MTL licensing just like fiat transmitters. Avoid unlicensed
operations in D.C. (Red).

Florida YELLOW
Recently Improved – Florida explicitly includes virtual
currency in MSB laws, but with a major exemption for P2P
transactions as of 2022. The Florida Supreme Court’s State v.
Espinoza (2019) initially held even a peer-to-peer Bitcoin sale
required a license . In response, Florida passed HB 273
(effective Jan 1, 2023) to clarify that only intermediaries are
money transmitters . Under the revised law, a license is
required only if one is acting as an intermediary that
transmits virtual currency for others (with the ability to
unilaterally execute or prevent a transfer) . Two-party
transactions (individual selling directly to another) no longer
require a license . Florida also removed virtual currency
from the definition of “payment instrument” and eased
reserve requirements for licensed crypto exchanges –
exchanges need only maintain like-kind crypto reserves equal
to outstanding obligations instead of extra fiat .
Conclusion: Florida is now crypto-friendly for direct
transactions, but a license is still needed for any
intermediary or platform business. Our approach: we can
send crypto to Florida clients for payment purposes (no third-
party intermediary) without making them or us a transmitter.
However, any service where we custody crypto for Florida
customers or exchange crypto-to-fiat on their behalf likely
requires a Florida money transmitter license.
Georgia RED
Included (Active Enforcement) – Georgia defines virtual
currency as a “digital representation of monetary value”
and clearly requires a license for crypto transmission . The
Department of Banking & Finance has issued cease-and-desist
orders against unlicensed crypto platforms (e.g. CampBX
exchange in 2018) . Georgia even empowered its regulator
to enact rules specifically for crypto transmitters . This is a
Red state – crypto = money transmission, enforced. We should
avoid Georgia unless licensed.

Hawaii GREEN
Newly Exempt – Historically, Hawaii was very restrictive
(requiring licensees to hold cash reserves equal to crypto
holdings, which led exchanges to exit). However, in Jan 2024
Hawaii announced it will no longer apply its Money
Transmitters Act to crypto . Following a 2019–2022
sandbox program study, Hawaii’s DFI concluded that typical
crypto exchange activities do not fit the definition of money
transmission . As a result, crypto companies no
longer need a Hawaii money transmitter license to do
business in the state . (They must still comply with federal
FinCEN regulations and other laws) . This change positions
Hawaii alongside Wyoming as explicitly crypto-exempt. We can
interact freely with Hawaiian clients in crypto, but note: if any
fiat transmission occurs, standard law would still apply (Hawaii
has adopted the CSBS Model Money Transmission
Modernization Act for fiat). For pure crypto though – Hawaii is
Green.
Idaho YELLOW
Case-by-Case Exemptions – Idaho’s default stance is that
crypto exchanges require a Money Transmitter license .
The Department of Finance explicitly lists “virtual currency
exchangers” as needing a license . However, Idaho
frequently issues No-Action Letters to exempt certain
business models: e.g. companies that sell crypto from their
own inventory (like one-way ATMs) have received no-action
relief . Idaho has also exempted trading platforms that
deal only in digital assets and do not handle fiat at all .
Essentially, if a business model avoids fiat currency entirely,
Idaho may allow it without a license via a no-action letter. In
practice, to operate in Idaho without a license, we would likely
need to request a no-action determination confirming our
specific crypto service is exempt. Because exemptions are not
automatic (they’re individualized), Idaho is categorized as
Yellow – a clear compliant path exists (license or no-action
exemption) but conditions apply.

Illinois GREEN
Guidance: Crypto Not Money – Illinois’ Transmitters of Money
Act does not clearly address digital currency. The state
regulator, however, issued guidance in 2017 stating “a person
engaged in the transmission of solely digital currencies…
would not be required to obtain” an Illinois license .
Only if a transaction involves both digital currency and fiat
money might it be considered money transmission,
depending on the structure . The Illinois DFPR has since
given numerous non-binding advisory letters to crypto
businesses confirming whether a license is needed .
Bottom line: In Illinois, crypto-only transfers (no fiat
involved) are NOT regulated as money transmission . If,
say, a client sends us Bitcoin and we forward Bitcoin, that’s
exempt. But if we exchange Bitcoin to USD on behalf of a
customer, that may trigger licensing – the Department asks
that businesses seek a determination in such mixed cases .
Illinois is thus Green for pure crypto activities. For any crypto-
to-fiat services touching Illinois residents, we would consult
DFPR for clarification, but simple crypto payments are safe to
perform without a license.
Indiana GREEN
Limited Scope – Exchanges Exempt – Indiana law’s money
transmitter definition doesn’t mention virtual currency .
The Indiana DFI has explicitly stated that no money
transmitter license is required for a business that simply
allows consumers to buy or sell virtual currency for fiat, as
long as the consumer cannot transmit fiat to another
person . In other words, a direct crypto exchange (user
trades dollars for Bitcoin or vice versa with the company)
is not considered money transmission in Indiana . Only
if the business were transmitting fiat on behalf of a consumer
to someone else would a license potentially be needed. This
policy makes Indiana a Green state for our use cases: we can
facilitate crypto buys/sells for Indiana clients under our own
ownership (no third-party fiat transfers) without licensing. If
we planned a service where we hold customer fiat to send to
others, then licensing would apply, but that’s outside pure
crypto scope.

Louisiana YELLOW
Virtual Currency Business Act – Louisiana enacted the
“Virtual Currency Businesses Act” (based on a model law)
effective 2020. Instead of using its standard money transmitter
law, Louisiana requires crypto companies to either register for
an exemption (if very limited activity) or obtain a virtual
currency business license from the Office of Financial

crypto (with requirements for cybersecurity, compliance, etc.),

exempts certain parties (e.g. banks, crypto miners, persons
using virtual currency for purchase of goods/services, and
small-scale or personal use). Given this structure, Louisiana is
Yellow: a license is needed for substantial crypto business, but
it’s a known process. For limited activities (like B2B crypto
transactions or technical, non-custodial support), exemptions
may apply by statute. We would likely avoid direct consumer
crypto services in Louisiana unless willing to go through the VC
Business license.
Maine RED
Included – Maine amended its money transmitter law to

currencies, for transmission” . The law states that any

Red (and as a smaller market, likely low priority for us).
Maryland RED

. No special exemptions have been announced (aside from
standard ones like banks). Maryland is thus Red – crypto
businesses need a license. We should avoid crypto
transmissions to or from Maryland residents unless licensed or
through a regulatory sandbox (Maryland had a Financial
Consumer Protection Act mandate to study crypto, but no
relief yet).

Unique Scope – Domestic Crypto Unregulated –

be “international money transmission” in the sense of the law

service also did not need a license . These suggest that as
long as fiat funds aren’t being sent abroad by the business,
Massachusetts does not regulate it. Thus, Massachusetts is
Green for most crypto activities – we can send crypto to MA
customers or receive crypto without licensing. Even a crypto-
to-fiat off-ramp for MA users likely doesn’t trigger MA law
unless it involves sending fiat out of the U.S. (which our use
cases do not). We should still comply with federal AML, but no
state MSB license is required for crypto transactions in MA.
Michigan RED
Included – Michigan law defines money transmission to
include receiving “money or monetary value” for transmission
. “Monetary value” would include cryptocurrencies as they
represent a medium of exchange. The Michigan DIFS has
explicitly noted that services like “holding funds in an e-wallet”
for others count as money transmission requiring a license
. Michigan has also updated other laws (like penal code
and tax guidance) to address crypto , indicating active
oversight. No exemption exists for crypto-only businesses.
Michigan is Red – do not engage without a license.
Minnesota RED

confirming that the state expects crypto businesses to be
licensed. No official guidance offering exemptions has been
published. Minnesota is considered Red (the presence of

inclusion). We would require a license to serve MN customers
in a custodial or intermediary capacity. Simple one-off crypto
payments to a MN recipient (not as a business) are not
regulated, but any business facilitation would be.

Included (with Crypto Bank Option) – Nebraska’s money

Pennsylvania – Crypto Not Money (Green)
Pennsylvania provides one of the most straightforward green lights. The PA Department of Banking and

considered transmitting money (even if crypto is used in the middle). But as long as the end product the

Authorized Delegate / Agent of an MSB: In traditional money transmission, a common model is an MSB
(licensed in many states) can appoint authorized delegates (agents) to conduct licensed activities under the
principal’s license. Many states allow this, requiring the principal to list agents with the state. If our sub-MSB
can be our authorized delegate, they might not need separate licensing in many states.
However, since we ourselves are not licensed in most states (except maybe home Montana, which doesn’t

does require a license (Yellow/Red), and we don’t hold one, then our sub-MSB cannot piggyback on us
(because we have no license to cover them with). We’d either need to get licensed in that state and then
appoint the sub-MSB as our agent, or the sub-MSB needs their own license.

d. Gift Card / Prepaid Card Funded by Crypto (Client sends crypto, we convert and load
fiat onto card)
This scenario mixes crypto and fiat and spans our services: 1. Client or end-user sends us cryptocurrency. 2.
We convert it (ourselves or via an exchange integration) to fiat currency. 3. We load that fiat onto a prepaid
card or gift card for the user, effectively giving them spendable USD (on, say, a Visa/MasterCard network).
From a regulatory standpoint, this touches both money transmission (we’re taking funds from a user and
delivering stored value) and possibly currency exchange (crypto to fiat conversion). And the prepaid card
issuing likely involves a program manager and a bank issuer. Typically, a prepaid program has a
sponsoring bank that issues the cards. That bank usually holds the stored funds, and our role is program
manager/loader.
State-by-state: - Many states treat the loading of a prepaid card as sale of a “payment instrument” or “stored
value” – which is an activity under money transmitter licensing. So if we receive money from a consumer to
load their prepaid card, we’d need a license (or an exemption). But here, the consumer isn’t sending us cash,
they’re sending crypto. Is that “money or monetary value” in each state? That’s the crux.
For Green states: If a user in a Green state sends crypto to load a prepaid card: - The act of sending crypto
to us isn’t regulated as money transmission (we established that). - However, when we then provide them a
card loaded with USD, we have effectively converted their crypto to fiat. In a sense, we are giving them a
“money service” – akin to an exchange or redemption. - The question: Do Green states consider that final
step (giving stored value for crypto) as money transmission or any regulated act? Since crypto isn’t money,

them stored value. One could frame it not as transmission but as a sale of stored value in exchange for
crypto. - Wyoming: Would likely be fine. They exempt crypto transmission . Also Wyoming does not
regulate selling a gift card for crypto as MSB because no fiat was received from the customer. If the card is
bank-issued, the bank’s involvement might preempt state rules anyway for that piece. - Montana: No MSB

stored value card by paying in crypto. Texas doesn’t license crypto payment, and selling a stored value card
might be considered selling a payment instrument – but since the payment instrument (the card) is
ultimately denominated in USD and can be used like money, selling that without a license could be an issue

covering money transmitters, by regulating issuers and potentially creating federal requirements for
wallets, it could reduce state oversight under the argument of federal occupation of the field. - The OCC’s
Special Purpose National Bank Charter for fintech (if revived and clarified for crypto) could let a company
like ours apply for a national license that preempts state MSB laws. This was attempted a few years ago, but
legal challenges stopped it. However, if either OCC or a new federal payments charter emerges (there’s talk
of a federal money transmitter license), that could be game-changing in a few years. Not something for
immediate reliance, but on our radar.
5. Enforcement Trends: - States are collaborating more on enforcement. E.g., multiple states jointly went
after BlockFi and Celsius (though that was more securities). For MSB, if one state finds an unlicensed crypto
operation (especially a Red state), they might alert others. With the NMLS network, information sharing is
easier. So the cost of being wrong in one state could lead to others piling on. This is why our conservative
approach is critical. - Some states like New Jersey, Texas, Alabama have been active in issuing cease and
desist for unregistered crypto interest accounts (like BlockFi) – not MSB per se, but shows regulators are
scanning the crypto industry. - Market events (FTX collapse, etc.) increased regulators’ resolve to enforce
existing laws. Even Green states might turn Yellow if a major incident occurs that makes them reconsider if
they need to monitor crypto more.

few specifically about MSB: - Alaska had a bill to update its money transmission law including virtual
currency – if that passes, Alaska likely goes from implicit include (as now) to explicit include (still Red). -

Summary of Trends: - Friendly states increasing modestly (especially smaller states, some big like FL and
TX doubling down on friendly interpretations). - Strict states also increasing efforts (NY, CA, a few others
standardizing). - Unified standards through CSBS model – likely to bring more states from ambiguous to

some state friction if it offers preemption.
For strategy, these trends mean: - Our Green list might expand a bit (WI, HI joining). - Some Orange will turn
Red (if they adopt model law) – so don’t rely on Orange as safe just because they haven’t spoken; they might
soon speak with a strict tone (e.g. ND surprise). - We should remain flexible: if a key market flips to Red and
is important (say, California now requiring license), we must consider obtaining that license or partnering to

Staying updated means regularly checking states’ legislative calendars and regulator announcements. We

Connecticut or Massachusetts could one day decide to formally regulate crypto (just speculation, but they
might if they see revenue opportunity and consumer risk). - Federal stablecoin law could overnight make
stablecoin transmissions something outside state control (for issuers, maybe not P2P use though).
Overall, trends require us to maintain a conservative baseline, but be ready to take advantage of new safe
harbors (like Hawaii’s change) and adjust to new requirements (like CA’s license by mid-2025).

Tier 1 – “Green Light” States: Prioritize and Actively Operate
These are states with explicit exemptions or no licensing for crypto (Green category). We should actively
focus our operations and client interactions in these jurisdictions, as they pose minimal regulatory
hurdles. Key Tier-1 states include: - Wyoming, Montana, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Texas, Illinois,
Virginia, South Carolina, Kansas, New Hampshire, Utah, and now Hawaii (post-2024) . Also
Massachusetts for domestic-only activities . - What to do: Emphasize marketing and onboarding in
these states. We can confidently send and receive crypto, offer off-ramp services, and allow sub-MSB series
to operate here. Ensure we still comply with any general consumer protection or AML obligations, but no
state license filings are needed. - Umbrella Strategy: We can set up series LLCs or operational hubs in
some of these states (we already have Montana). E.g., forming a Wyoming series for custody or stablecoin
issuance could leverage WY’s favorable laws (like SPDIs if needed). A Texas presence could help with
banking relationships and user trust, given TX’s clarity (though not legally required, a TX entity might
reassure partners). - Watch list: Keep these Green states under review for any legislative changes (low
probability of negative change, but for example if federal law prompts them to adjust definitions, etc., we
should catch it early).
Tier 2 – “Proceed with Caution” States: Conditional Operations or Licensing
These Yellow states are safe for operations if structured correctly or if we obtain certain licenses. Our
approach: - Operate with Structure: In states like Florida (post-HB273), North Carolina, Idaho (with no-
action letters), Arkansas (no-action letters), and Colorado (with limited model) , we will operate
but ensuring we meet their conditions: - Use agent-of-payee agreements for payment processing in NC
(and similar states with that exemption) . - Design transactions in Florida as two-party exchanges
whenever possible (so we are either the buyer or seller principal in the crypto transaction with the customer,
not an intermediary) . - In states like Idaho or Arkansas, if a sub-MSB or we plan a certain activity (e.g.
crypto ATM or limited exchange), proactively seek a no-action letter confirming exemption . This gives
written security to operate without a license. - Use Sandbox programs if available (e.g., Utah’s, Florida’s,
West Virginia’s) for testing new offerings in Yellow states with minimal risk . - Licensing Path if Needed:

Rhode Island – Getting a currency transmission license (with the crypto addendum) in RI is relatively
straightforward in NMLS. If we see business potential in RI (or any state that adopted the ULC model), we
can consider licensing. One license may cover both fiat and crypto activities. - Louisiana – If we want to
serve LA customers, we might apply for the Virtual Currency Business license or file for the exemption if
volume is low. Louisiana’s requirements are moderate (financials, background checks, compliance program).

ease partnering with banks and satisfying B2B counterparties. Essentially, having at least one or a few state
licenses as a demonstration of compliance-mindedness could be worthwhile, even if not strictly needed in

Tier 3 – “High Risk/Avoid” States: No-go without full compliance (Red)
These are the Red states where crypto is clearly regulated and enforcement is active. Our recommendation:
- Avoid offering services to residents of these states unless and until we obtain proper licensing. In
practical terms, geofence our app/website to block sign-ups or transactions involving addresses or bank
accounts in these states. (At least for retail. Institutional dealings we handle case-by-case with legal input.) -
Red states to avoid in near-term: New York, California (starting July 2025), Georgia, New Jersey (until
any new law perhaps provides a framework), Washington, Connecticut, Kentucky, Alabama, South
Dakota, Kansas (though Kansas is Green actually – scratch Kansas here), Ohio, Michigan, and others
listed as Red in matrix. Essentially any state with explicit inclusion and no exemption. - Maintain a “Do Not
Touch” list for our compliance team of states we do not onboard customers from, and ensure transactions
aren’t knowingly involving those jurisdictions. - If there is significant business opportunity in a Red state
that justifies cost (e.g., a large client base in New York or California we don’t want to ignore): - Develop a
plan to get licensed in that state. That might mean hiring compliance personnel, securing surety bond, etc.
For NY, maybe down the road if our volume warrants, we apply for BitLicense (long process). For CA,
perhaps by late 2025 we apply for the DFAL license if we want that market – note it takes effect July 2025,
they might not enforce strictly until companies have had time to apply. - Alternatively, encourage our
institutional clients in those states to be the ones who handle regulated pieces. For instance, if we have a
partner in NY with a BitLicense, route NY customer transactions to be serviced by that partner (revenue
share) instead of directly by us. - Keep Red states in our long-term roadmap: e.g., aim to be in New York by
obtaining a BitLicense by Year 3 if we plan a nationwide presence, but only after building enough scale to
afford it. Similarly, aim for California license by early 2026 as it’s huge (the law may require it anyway by
then to not shut out CA users).

our model likely doesn’t require a license. This can help if later a regulator questions us; it shows good faith.

states require surety bonds; we might voluntarily secure a blanket bond that could also apply if we later
quickly need to register somewhere (some bonding companies offer nationwide MSB bonds). - Keep our
compliance program at bank-grade level even if not required by state law in Green areas. That means
strong AML, transaction monitoring, cybersecurity – this not only prepares us for easier licensing later if
needed but also can be a factor in regulators granting no-action or leniency (they see we self-regulate well).
Summary Tiered Approach:
Focus resources on Green states (Tier 1) to grow business without licensing friction. Yellow states (Tier 2)
– operate but design around their rules or selectively become licensed, using partnerships to fill gaps.
Orange states – hold off or treat as needing license pending clarity; do not invest heavily there until more
info. Red states (Tier 3) – exclude for now in our offerings to avoid legal issues, but have a plan for eventual
entry (through licensing or partnership) for any that are too commercially significant to ignore (like
California by late 2025, New York in long run).
By following this strategy, we maintain a conservative compliance profile – we do not “push boundaries”
where regulators haven’t given a green light. This minimizes regulatory risk (enforcement actions, fines,
forced shutdowns), which is critical given our low-risk philosophy. Instead, we concentrate our US
footprint in clearly permissive jurisdictions, thereby avoiding costly legal battles and fostering goodwill
with banking partners and regulators.
Finally, we will revisit this analysis regularly (at least quarterly) to update categories and ensure no new laws
have turned a once-Green state into Yellow/Red or vice versa. By staying agile and informed, we can expand
our services confidently yet cautiously across the U.S. compliance patchwork.
