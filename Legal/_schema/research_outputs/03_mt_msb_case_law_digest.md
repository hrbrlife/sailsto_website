Note: Montana is unique in that it does not require a state money transmitter license, meaning Money
Services Businesses (MSBs) can operate without a Montana license . However, MSBs must still comply
with federal law, including FinCEN registration and anti-money-laundering (AML) programs under the
Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) . The cases above illustrate how both federal and state authorities have
addressed unlicensed money transmission, fraud schemes involving money movement, and money
laundering with a Montana nexus in the last 20 years.
B. Red Flags & Patterns in Montana Financial Crime Litigation
1. Unlicensed Money Transmission & Lack of Registration: Because Montana has no state licensure for
money transmitters, unscrupulous actors may attempt to use the state as a “safe haven.” In U.S. v. Chabot,
the defendant ran an unregistered money transfer business ( Avalanche Creek LLC ) that funneled fraud
proceeds overseas . She never obtained a state license (none required in MT) nor registered
properly with FinCEN, violating 18 U.S.C. § 1960. Chabot pleaded guilty to operating an “unlicensed money
transmitting business” and multiple laundering counts . Likewise, in U.S. v. Rule, a Montana resident
helped convert scam funds to crypto without implementing any MSB compliance – the DOJ noted they
“willfully violated the money services business requirements of the [BSA]” . Red flag: Businesses or persons
moving money for others without any licensing or FinCEN registration. Consequence: Federal prosecutors
will charge 18 U.S.C. § 1960 (unlicensed MSB) even if Montana law doesn’t require a license, by relying on
the federal registration/AML obligations .
2. Multiple Bank Account Closures & Ignoring Warnings: A striking pattern is the misuse of bank
accounts and banks shutting them down. In the Chabot case, the defendant opened 50+ bank accounts to
receive scam funds; “Banks closed numerous accounts after reviewing the activity, and at least 15 banks directly
informed Chabot the accounts were being closed because of fraud…Despite repeated account closures, Chabot
opened new bank accounts” to continue the scheme . This shows a recurring red flag: an individual or
entity has a history of account closures for suspicious activity. In litigation, evidence of prior closures
and warnings (“account closed due to fraud”) was damning . Despite these clear signals, Chabot
persisted – a fact the court likely viewed as proof of willfulness. Red flag: Client has a pattern of banks
closing accounts for cause (fraud/AML reasons). Takeaway: Financial institutions and enforcement note
that continuing to open new accounts after closures indicates knowing engagement in illicit activity. An MSB
should avoid any business that has been de-banked repeatedly and report such patterns.
3. Commissions or “Fees” for Money Movement (Money Mule Indicators): The Chabot case also
illustrated the classic money mule structure. She took ~10% commissions from victim funds as payment for
moving money . For example, if $10,000 from a romance scam victim hit her account, she’d keep $1k and
wire $9k to overseas co-conspirators. This fee-taking pattern – a round-percentage cut on each transfer –
is a strong red flag that the intermediary is an illicit money broker. Courts have highlighted how “mules often
receive a commission for providing their services” . Red flag: Account holder consistently retains a small
percentage and forwards the balance elsewhere. Litigation outcome: Taking a cut underscored Chabot’s
knowledge and profit motive, supporting fraud and money laundering convictions.
4. Mislabeling Transactions & False Representations: In the Rule crypto laundering case, the defendants
went to lengths to disguise the nature of transactions. They “provided instructions to co-conspirators and
victims to label wire transfers as ‘loan repayments’ and ‘advertising’” to avoid detection of fraud . They
also lied on account opening documents and in communications with banks and exchanges . These
deceptive descriptors and false info were crucial evidence of intent to conceal. Red flags: Payments with

5. Sending Funds to High-Risk/Unrelated Recipients or Jurisdictions: Another recurring theme is money
flowing to unrelated third parties or high-risk regions without legitimate purpose. Chabot’s operation sent
funds to the United Arab Emirates (Dubai) frequently , which in context was a destination for global
fraud rings. In Lefthand (the Montana drug ring case), the defendant wired drug proceeds to a supplier in
Washington state . In many consumer fraud cases, money is sent via MSBs to overseas scammers (e.g.
West Africa, China, Eastern Europe). Red flag: A customer who regularly transfers money to far-flung locales
or individuals with no obvious business/personal ties, especially if coupled with other risk factors (e.g. elderly
sender, recent large deposits). In Western Union’s 2017 settlement, DOJ noted WU’s system “facilitated
scammers and rip-offs” by allowing relentless overseas transfers from victims . MSBs are now obligated to
monitor for atypical corridors and report suspicious flows. Courts and regulators alike view blind eye
facilitation of such transfers as willful AML failure .
6. Fraudulent Documents & Identity Concealment: Several cases show offenders using fraudulent
documents or false identities to further schemes. In State v. Brandt, the Ponzi schemer gave investors fake
business agreements and no prospectus . In U.S. v. Moore, the defendant made false statements to
both victims and the FBI (resulting in a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1001) . Using straw account holders
or complicit agents is another tactic (e.g. Western Union agents who colluded with fraudsters – 26 agents
were convicted since 2001) . Red flags: Documents that are obviously altered or inconsistent, customers
who conceal their identity or use multiple IDs. In litigation, these forgeries or misrepresentations often lead
to aggravated charges (e.g. Moore received an obstruction sentencing enhancement for perjury ).
Financial institutions must verify KYC info; discrepancies can indicate deeper fraud.
7. Abuse of Trust and Vulnerable Victims: Montana cases frequently involve elderly or vulnerable
victims and trust abuse. Brandt targeted mostly elderly investors (many from his church or insurance
clientele) with a “too good to be true” house-flipping investment . Chabot laundered money from
romance scams and COVID-19 grant fraud – crimes that prey on trust. Western Union’s enforcement
highlighted “abuse of vulnerable populations” as the company’s lax controls enabled mass marketing fraud
. Red flag: A client’s funds come in as multiple personal checks or payments from different individuals
(especially seniors) not obviously related – could be victims pooling into a fraud. Also, a professional
fiduciary (lawyer, broker, etc.) moving client money in odd ways. Courts impose harsher sentences when
trust is abused (Moore’s case discussed an *“abuse of trust” enhancement, though ultimately overturned as
the victims weren’t in a formal trust relationship at solicitation ). MSBs should treat unusual
management of others’ funds, especially from those at risk (elderly, etc.), as a red flag requiring scrutiny or
reporting.
8. Large Cash Transactions & Structuring Behavior: In drug-related cases like Lefthand’s, cash from street
sales had to be aggregated to send to suppliers. DOJ noted one Montana ring laundered tens of thousands
through a bank account and money orders . Often this is done by structuring – breaking deposits
into <$10k chunks to avoid Currency Transaction Reports. While specific Montana cases on structuring
weren’t highlighted in media, it’s a common issue in drug and gambling-related prosecutions. Red flag:

Federal law (31 U.S.C. § 5324) makes structuring a crime, and any MSB or bank caught facilitating it (even
unwittingly) faces penalties. FinCEN Geographic Targeting Orders (like a recent 2025 GTO for border areas)
dramatically lowered reporting thresholds (to $200) to catch structuring in money services . This
underscores that regulators expect MSBs to monitor for even small cash patterns in certain scenarios.
Montana businesses near borders or serving cash-heavy clients must be alert to structuring signs or risk
being swept into enforcement (indeed, a lawsuit Valuta v. FinCEN in 2025 challenges such an order’s
burden on small MSBs).
Summary: Across these cases, repeated red flags include unlicensed operations, rapid opening/closing of
accounts, taking commissions on transfers, falsifying transfer details, unusual fund flows to high-risk
areas, use of false documents, exploitation of trust, and structuring cash. Montana’s laissez-faire
regulatory environment for MSBs has not prevented federal and state authorities from aggressively
prosecuting those red flags when they manifest in fraud or laundering schemes. On the contrary, the
absence of a state license requirement heightens the need for Montana MSBs to vigilantly self-regulate
under federal standards – as these cases demonstrate, “no state license needed” does not mean “no
rules apply.”
C. Mapping Red Flags to Automated Detection Controls
Financial institutions and MSBs can translate the above red flags into specific, automated compliance
checks. Below is a mapping of common red flags (as distilled from Montana-related cases) to potential
“agentic” verification/detection rules:
Unregistered MSB Activity: If an individual or business processes payments or transfers above a
certain volume/frequency without an MSB license on record, flag for review. Detection: Cross-check
customer names against FinCEN’s MSB registration list. For Montana (no state license), ensure at
least FinCEN registration exists for any entity moving, say, >$X in third-party funds monthly. Lack of
registration where required should trigger an alert (as in Chabot’s case – operating “an unregistered
money transmitting business” ).
Multiple Account Closures / Institution Hopping: If a new customer’s account is closed by the
bank’s AML system (or if the customer abruptly ceases after inquiry), and the same individual
reappears opening another account (especially at a different branch or affiliated institution) – flag it.
Detection: Maintain an internal “closed for cause” watchlist. An agentic verifier could use consortium
data (if available) to see if the person had accounts closed at other banks. In Chabot’s scenario, an AI
could detect that “this applicant previously had 5 accounts closed in last 2 years for suspicious activity” –
a risk score would spike, prompting human review or denial. Automated tracking of repeated
suspicious closures would have identified her earlier pattern .
Commission/Skimming Pattern: Implement algorithms to detect when an account’s outgoing
transfers consistently equal a fixed percentage (e.g. ~90%) of incoming amounts. Detection: For
each customer, compute the ratio of outflows to inflows over short windows. If 85–95% of funds are
sent out and 5–15% retained (especially if this repeats for multiple cycles), mark as potential money
mule receiving commissions. This mirrors Chabot’s ~10% fee pattern . The system might cross-
flag this alongside diverse payers/payees to confirm it’s not just normal income/expense behavior.

Misleading Transaction Descriptions: Use natural language processing on wire and payment
memos to flag suspicious keywords or inconsistencies. Detection: Flag instances of personal
remittances with business-sounding notes (“loan repayment,” “consulting fee”) or vice versa. An
agent could also learn common scam narrative terms. In Rule’s case, numerous wires labeled
“advertising” going to personal accounts would be a clear anomaly . The system could alert on
keywords when context (customer profile, known relationship) doesn’t match – prompting
investigation into the true purpose.
Unusual Recipient Patterns / Geographies: Establish profiles for typical customer transaction
corridors. Detection: If a Montana customer suddenly starts wiring funds to, say, Dubai or Nigeria
with no prior history or plausible reason, generate an alert. Similarly, track consolidation: many small
incoming credits from around the U.S., followed by a few large outgoing international wires is a red
flag pattern (seen in many scams). Agentic verifiers can employ geo-risk scoring: e.g., transfers to
countries with high fraud/money laundering risk (based on FATF lists or internal typologies) get extra
scrutiny. In Lefthand’s case, frequent wires from rural Montana to an urban area in Washington
outside of known family ties would flag . A rules-based system could require either a CTR/SAR or
a pause for review on such out-of-profile flows.
Frequency and Velocity Checks: Many Montana schemes involved rapid, repeated movements of
funds (often just below reporting thresholds). Detection: Use velocity metrics – e.g., more than X
transfers in Y days, or aggregate cash in/out exceeding certain norms for that customer type – to
identify structuring or laundering. For example, if an MSB agent sees a customer send $9,500 in cash
transfers five times in two weeks, an automated rule should flag structuring. Western Union’s
compliance program now incorporates such velocity checks as part of its enhanced obligations .
An AI monitor would not only catch outright structuring, but adapt to FinCEN orders (like the $200
threshold GTO ) by dynamically adjusting thresholds in designated areas.
False ID or Document Detection: Implement document verification (e.g., image analysis to spot
tampering) and identity link analysis. Detection: If different accounts or transactions share a
common ID number or address but under different names, the system should alert potential
synthetic identity or “smurf” network. An example rule: flag if the same driver’s license is used by
multiple customers. In Moore’s case, while not about fake IDs, his false statements would have been
caught earlier if the system cross-checked provided info (e.g., no felony history) against public
records . Automated public records checks for things like criminal history (where legally
permissible) or negative news could have flagged Blair lying on his EIDL application. A robust agentic
verifier would integrate external data to catch discrepancies – for instance, Blair claimed no recent
felonies, but an automated criminal record scan would contradict that .
Elderly Victim Payment Alerts: Train models to recognize when a likely elderly customer is sending
large or repeated wires, especially to new payees. Detection: Use date of birth data to tag senders
over a certain age, then monitor if they are sending to individuals unknown to them (not family) or to
businesses that sound like sweepstakes, tech support, etc. If an 80-year-old in Montana suddenly
wires $50,000 to a Florida account referencing “investment,” that should prompt a fraud interdiction
call. Western Union and MoneyGram both now have interdiction questionnaires for agents when
seniors attempt unusual transfers – an automated system can backstop this by analyzing network-
wide patterns (e.g., dozens of seniors sending to the same receiver name – as happened in the WU
fraud cases). Such a system might have caught patterns early that led to Western Union’s failure;

Cash Structural Anomalies: For banks in Montana, an automated BSA system should flag cash
transactions that appear structured. Detection: If a business that normally has no cash deposits
suddenly starts making frequent $9,000 deposits, or customers purchase multiple money orders
under $3,000 at different locations on the same day, the system groups and flags them. In the
Montana drug case (Lefthand), agents observed tens of thousands laundered through a single
account – an AI could note that the volume of cash credits was inconsistent with any stated business
activity on that account, prompting a SAR . Modern AML software uses both rules and machine
learning to spot when cash activity doesn’t match the customer’s peer group profile.
Bottom line: The “red flags” seen repeatedly in Montana-related enforcement can be codified into detection
rules. An agentic verifier (AI compliance agent) should aggregate behavior across accounts, time, and
institutions (if data-sharing is available) to identify when normal patterns are broken in ways that match
these typologies. By automating the recognition of these red flags – and escalating alerts for human
investigation – MSBs and banks can avoid the fate of being cited in the next case law example of compliance
failure. As Western Union learned, ignoring obvious red flags can lead to massive penalties for “willfully
failing to maintain an effective AML program” . Conversely, early detection and intervention (e.g., stopping
a fraudulent wire, filing a timely SAR) not only protects victims but also keeps the institution out of court
except as an assisting witness.
D. Source Extracts from Key Cases and Authorities
Below are selected authoritative excerpts (with citations) from case opinions and official documents,
providing the full context behind the summarized red flags and legal outcomes:
Montana’s Non-Regulation of Money Transmitters: “Montana is one of the few states in the country
that does not maintain a money transmitter regime… the Montana website stated ‘MONTANA DOES NOT
REGULATE MONEY TRANSMITTERS. At this time, Montana does not regulate money transmitters. There is no
licensing requirement in Montana for money transmitters.’” (Thus MSBs in Montana rely solely on
federal compliance).
Chabot Indictment (Money Mule Scheme): “The indictment alleges that from about May 2016 until
about February 2021, Chabot conspired with others and through her business, Avalanche Creek, LLC, an
unregistered money transmitting business, to operate as a money mule…Banks closed numerous accounts
after reviewing the activity, and at least 15 banks directly informed Chabot the accounts were being closed
because of fraud…Despite repeated account closures, Chabot opened new bank accounts…Chabot received
a fee, which in many instances totaled approximately 10 percent of the fraudulent proceeds…Chabot
would then forward the remaining funds overseas, including to Dubai, in the United Arab Emirates.”
Chabot Conviction (18 U.S.C. 1960): “Theresa Anne Chabot appeals… following her guilty-plea
conviction for conducting an unlicensed money transmitting business, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1960.”
(Ninth Circuit confirming the conviction under the federal unlicensed MSB statute).

Chairwoman Ramirez… The agreements… ensure Western Union changes the way it conducts business and
provides more than a half billion dollars for refunds to consumers…’” . (WU’s deferred
prosecution exemplifies how regulators penalize MSBs that ignore red flags of fraud and money
laundering.)
